Ardern tells Sydney Opera House forum politicians must rebuild trust through financial security

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Jacinda Ardern used a Sydney Opera House forum with the author Anna Funder on Sunday to argue that politicians are aggravating social polarisation and should respond to public insecurity with policies that build financial security rather than with blame. "We have a responsibility not to engage in binary thinking, not to engage in the language of violence, [but] to rebuild trust by building financial security in a time of deep insecurity," the former New Zealand prime minister said, according to The Guardian.[S18]

Ardern's argument, as reported, links economic anxiety to the deterioration of political trust. She said that over the past few decades people around the world had felt an increasing sense of insecurity as globalisation took hold, and that they began attacking institutions such as the World Trade Organization. Politicians responded to that insecurity with proposals like Brexit, she said, offering voters a target — "Here's what you blame" — which in her account then paved the way for the rise of leaders such as Donald Trump.[S18]

That sequence is Ardern's interpretation of recent political history rather than a settled causal account, and the reporting presents it as her remarks at the forum. Its relevance to her broader message is that she treats insecurity as the raw material that polarising politics feeds on, and treats the offer of a scapegoat as the mechanism by which mainstream politics can hand advantage to movements it later struggles to contain. The claim is contestable on its own terms: attributing Brexit and Trump's rise to a single driver of economic insecurity compresses a much wider set of causes, and the report does not present her as claiming otherwise. What the remarks do establish is how she frames the problem for a centre-left audience — that the demand side of populism is material, and that the supply side is a choice politicians make about whom to blame.[S18]

The appearance also gave Ardern a platform to revisit her own experience with social media, a subject on which she described a sharp change in view. When she entered politics in 2008, she was not only the youngest sitting MP in the New Zealand parliament but also the first to arrive with an existing social media account. At the time, many politicians — Ardern included — were enthusiastic about using the platforms to communicate directly with voters. "I was so naive not to think about the fact that that meant the manipulation of information," she said.[S18]

Ardern's post-political work has kept her in that debate. She has been a prominent voice on countering violent extremism online and on the responsibilities of technology companies, and her remarks in Sydney fit that strand of advocacy. The Guardian's report does not detail any new initiative, proposal or commitment announced at the event; the substance lies in her diagnosis of political trust and her account of how online radicalisation changed her own assumptions about direct digital communication.[S18]

The forum format — a conversation with a novelist rather than a policy address — is worth noting for how the argument was framed. Ardern moved between the macro level of globalisation, trade institutions and electoral backlash and the personal level of her own early optimism about social media. The connecting thread in her remarks is that institutions and leaders underestimated how quickly insecurity and information manipulation could be converted into political volatility. Funder, whose work has examined surveillance and the state's reach into private life, provides a setting in which those themes sit naturally, though the report does not describe her questions or contributions in detail.[S18]

For Australian and New Zealand audiences, the setting carries its own context. Ardern remains a widely recognised figure in both countries, and her post-leadership commentary is followed closely by people interested in how centre-left governments handle trust, inequality and platform accountability. The Guardian's report does not indicate whether her remarks were directed at any particular government or opposition, and there is no indication in the supplied material of a direct response from Australian or New Zealand political figures. The Sydney Opera House venue gives the appearance the character of a public cultural event rather than a party or parliamentary platform, which tends to widen reach while lowering the expectation of immediate policy consequence.[S18]

The reported remarks also sit within a longer argument about what governments can realistically do. Ardern's prescription — building financial security as a route to rebuilding trust — implies that communication strategy or appeals to civility alone are insufficient if voters feel economically exposed. That is a claim about priorities and causation, not a finding established by the reporting, and the forum account does not include the specific policies she would attach to it. The distinction matters for how the speech is likely to be used: as a rhetorical frame available to governments arguing that cost-of-living support is trust-building, rather than as a detailed programme with measurable commitments.[S18]

Her account of the Christchurch attack also carries a narrower, more concrete implication. By describing the attacker as radicalised on YouTube and broadcasting on Facebook, Ardern points to two distinct regulatory problems — discovery and distribution — that have been handled unevenly. Recommendation and discovery systems determine what users are shown; livestreaming and upload infrastructure determine how fast violent material spreads. The report does not say whether she proposed new rules on either front on Sunday, and the absence of a specific ask is itself part of what the event establishes: this was diagnosis and reflection, not a regulatory launch.[S18]

What the report does establish is narrower and clearer: Ardern told a Sydney audience that politicians bear responsibility for avoiding binary thinking and violent language, that she regards financial security as central to restoring trust, and that her own confidence in social media as a force for direct democratic communication was undone by the Christchurch attack and the radicalisation pathway that preceded it. The event adds to a running conversation among former leaders about why trust in institutions has eroded and what, if anything, mainstream politics can do about it. Ardern's contribution is to insist that the answer is partly material — people need to feel secure — and partly behavioural, in how politicians choose to speak about opponents and blame. Whether that argument gains traction with sitting governments is a separate question the forum did not resolve, and the report offers no evidence of how the audience or political class received it.[S18]

Ardern is arguing that economic insecurity, not just disinformation, is the fuel for polarisation — a claim that shifts responsibility toward governments' redistributive and social-insurance choices rather than toward platform moderation alone. For centre-left parties in New Zealand, Australia and the UK, that framing offers a defence of spending on security and cost-of-living support as trust-building rather than mere welfare. It also gives tech platforms a partial reprieve: if insecurity drives backlash, content rules address symptoms. The Christchurch passage keeps platform accountability on the table, but the emphasis is on politicians' language and economic record.
