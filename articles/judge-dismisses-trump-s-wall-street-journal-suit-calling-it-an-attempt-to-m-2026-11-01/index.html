A federal judge has dismissed President Donald Trump's defamation lawsuit against The Wall Street Journal over its reporting on a sexually suggestive letter and drawing the paper said bore Trump's name, ruling that the complaint attempted to "manufacture" flaws in the Journal's investigative reporting, NPR reported. The decision marked the second time the case had been thrown out, and it landed as the president and his administration remain engaged in litigation with several other news organizations.[S9]

The dispute centers on a story the Journal published last year describing a letter the paper said was included in a 2003 birthday album compiled for Jeffrey Epstein. According to the Journal's account, the letter bearing Trump's name includes text framed by the outline of what appears to be a hand-drawn naked woman and ends with the line, "Happy Birthday — and may every day be another wonderful secret." The album was reportedly collected by Ghislaine Maxwell, the disgraced British socialite, as part of a birthday tribute to Epstein years before he was first arrested in 2006 and before he and Trump had a falling out, the reporting said.[S9]

The case had already been dismissed once. Judge Gayles dismissed the suit in April, finding that Trump had failed to make the argument that the article was published with the intent to be malicious. The judge then gave the president a chance to file an amended complaint, which he did in May. The new ruling rejected that amended version as well, with Gayles also saying several other allegations in the lawsuit did not have merit. The judge's language about manufacturing flaws suggests the court viewed the complaint as an attempt to construct legal defects in reporting the paper stood behind, rather than as a genuine dispute over whether the underlying facts were wrong.[S9]

The legal bar in the case is the one set by New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, the 1964 Supreme Court decision that made it harder for public figures to win libel lawsuits. Under that standard, a public figure must prove that a media outlet knowingly published something false or showed a reckless disregard for the truth. That requirement has shaped the case from the start, and the judge's finding that the amended complaint did not clear it is the core of the dismissal. The standard is demanding by design: it protects reporting on public figures even when it contains errors, unless the plaintiff can show the outlet knew the material was false or harbored serious doubts about its accuracy.[S9]

The ruling lands in a broader pattern of litigation between Trump and news organizations during his second term. In addition to the Journal, he is embroiled in lawsuits involving the BBC and The Associated Press. Most recently, CNN, MS NOW and Politico have sued him for restored access after he banished them from the White House in mid-September. NPR reported that the dismissal is a blow to Trump and his administration, which have both pursued adversarial relationships with news organizations during his second term.[S9]

The Journal case is unusual in that it turns on a specific piece of documentary evidence — a letter the paper says it reviewed and described — rather than on a dispute over access or sourcing alone. That distinction matters for how the remaining cases proceed: a ruling that a public figure failed to meet the Sullivan standard in one case does not automatically decide others, but it does signal how courts may treat similar complaints. The access lawsuits brought by CNN, MS NOW and Politico raise different legal questions, centered on whether the White House can exclude outlets from press facilities, and they will move through their own procedural stages independent of the Journal ruling.[S9]

What the dismissal does not resolve is the underlying factual dispute over the letter itself. The ruling is a procedural and legal judgment about the sufficiency of the complaint, not a finding that the Journal's reporting was accurate or inaccurate. The Sullivan standard has been a fixture of U.S. libel law for six decades, and its application here follows a familiar pattern: public figures suing over coverage of their conduct face a high bar, and courts have repeatedly dismissed complaints that allege reputational harm without specific evidence of knowing falsity or reckless disregard. The Journal case fits that pattern, with the judge finding that even after an opportunity to amend, the complaint did not clear the threshold. That outcome is consistent with how similar suits against major outlets have fared, though each case turns on its own facts and pleadings.[S9]

For the Journal, the ruling closes a legal chapter over a story that drew intense attention because of its subject matter and the identities involved. The paper has not indicated any change to its reporting posture, and the dismissal does not require it to retract or alter the story. For the president, the loss is one of several legal fronts involving the press, and the decision on whether to appeal will likely be weighed against the resources and attention required by the other cases. The access lawsuits and the defamation cases are distinct legal tracks, but they share a common question about the limits of presidential power over news organizations. Courts have generally protected editorial judgment and press access, though the specific outcomes depend on the facts and the legal theories advanced. The Journal ruling adds one more data point to that body of decisions, and it reinforces the difficulty of winning a defamation suit against a major outlet over reporting a public figure disputes.[S9]
