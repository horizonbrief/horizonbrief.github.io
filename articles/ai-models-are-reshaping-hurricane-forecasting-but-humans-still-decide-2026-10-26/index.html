AI Models Are Reshaping Hurricane Forecasting, but Humans Still Decide

NPR reports that DeepMind's model outperformed rivals on Isaias' track but trailed the official blended forecast on intensity. Human forecasters remain central to interpreting the results.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

When Hurricane Isaias came ashore near Destin, Florida, in early October as a Category 2 storm, NPR described an early divergence between AI and conventional projections of its landfall. According to NPR, traditional physics-based models initially projected the storm would track farther west, toward southeastern Louisiana. Google DeepMind's artificial intelligence model, by contrast, called the western Florida Panhandle from the outset — a divergence that illustrates how machine learning is beginning to change the practice of hurricane prediction.[S1]

Michael Lowry, a longtime Miami-based meteorologist and hurricane expert who has previously advised both the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the National Hurricane Center, told NPR that DeepMind's system has established itself as a benchmark. "Google DeepMind, in particular, has really proven itself the last few years as the gold standard model," Lowry said. The National Hurricane Center began incorporating the DeepMind model into its official forecasts last year, and by the end of the 2025 Atlantic hurricane season it had already become one of the strongest performers at predicting both a storm's track and its strength, according to Brian McNoldy, a hurricane researcher at the University of Miami.[S1]

The shift matters because the two approaches to forecasting work in fundamentally different ways. Traditional models ingest real-time atmospheric observations — temperature, humidity, pressure and wind shear collected by satellites and aircraft — and run them through complex physical equations to project where a storm will travel and how powerful it will become. AI models lean more heavily on history, identifying patterns across thousands of past storms paired with atmospheric data. The experts interviewed by NPR also described uncertainty about how the AI systems reach their answers. Lowry said forecasters cannot always explain either their successes or their mistakes.[S1]

NPR describes neural networks as algorithms trained to find patterns in large datasets. Lowry noted that many developers are software engineers rather than meteorologists. The report distinguishes those development teams from the meteorologists responsible for interpreting model outputs and issuing operational forecasts.[S1]

The landscape of AI weather models is uneven. Lowry said there are many unproven weather forecasting AI models in circulation, and he distinguished those systems from established performers. The two that have performed best so far, despite some shortcomings, are those developed by Google DeepMind and by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, the institution known as ECMWF.[S1]

At the National Hurricane Center, forecasters do not rely on any single system. Director Michael Brennan said the agency draws on several proven models, both AI and traditional, when assembling its official forecasts. Brennan explained that each storm presents different challenges, which is why the agency combines the strongest available model guidance rather than selecting a single system. The human role extends beyond model selection. The NHC issues updates every six hours during active hurricanes, while human forecasters interpret the model outputs. Forecasters must translate those shifting outputs into clear, actionable guidance for the public.[S1]

Isaias itself exposed where the AI approach still falls short. Brennan said that while the DeepMind model outperformed other models in tracking the storm's path, it did not match the NHC's blended forecast in projecting the storm's intensity, or strength. His team provides regular feedback to Google DeepMind to help refine the model; Google did not respond to questions from NPR for the story.[S1]

Brennan noted that errors in intensity forecasting are now about half of what they were around the time of Hurricane Katrina, a measure of how far the field has come through improvements in observations and forecasting models.[S1]

What excites Lowry about AI is the pace of improvement. Traditional forecasting models are updated annually at best, while AI systems can be revised in months or even weeks. That cadence could allow forecasters to absorb improvements far more quickly than the multi-year cycles that govern conventional numerical weather prediction.[S1]

Still, the NPR reporting makes clear that AI has not replaced the established forecasting enterprise. The NHC's approach remains a consensus blend, and humans interpret the results before anything reaches the public. The DeepMind model's success with Isaias' track came alongside a weaker showing on intensity, a reminder that different models can excel at different tasks within the same storm. The agency's practice of feeding performance data back to developers suggests an iterative relationship in which operational forecasters help shape the tools they use.[S1]

Why it matters

Hurricane forecasts drive evacuation orders, storm-surge planning and public safety decisions along U.S. coastlines. If AI models can call landfall location earlier and more accurately than physics-based systems, as the Isaias case suggests, they could extend warning lead times. But the same reporting shows the leading AI model lagged on intensity and operates as a black box, so the value depends on how carefully forecasters blend and interpret it.

What happens next

The National Hurricane Center is expected to keep running AI and traditional models side by side through upcoming Atlantic seasons, feeding performance feedback to developers like Google DeepMind. Watch whether AI models close the intensity gap and whether more AI systems earn a place in the NHC's consensus blend.

What to watch

  • NHC track and intensity error scores for the DeepMind and ECMWF AI models versus the blended official forecast in the next Atlantic season
  • Whether the DeepMind model improves on intensity prediction after NHC feedback
  • Any expansion of AI models beyond DeepMind and ECMWF into the NHC's operational consensus
  • Public explanation from developers about how AI models reach their forecasts, addressing the black-box concern
  • Performance of AI models on storms with unusual tracks or rapid intensification, where historical patterns may mislead
