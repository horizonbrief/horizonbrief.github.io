Italian Farmer Revives an Ancient Wheat Found in His Attic, and It May Outlast Modern Varieties

Claudio Grossi planted unidentified seeds left by his great-grandfather and grew a tall, drought-tolerant grain now sold as grano del miracolo. Its revival raises a practical question for a warming Mediterranean: whether old genetics can do work that modern wheat cannot.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

In the early 1990s, Claudio Grossi, then a 40-year-old farmer in Parma in northern Italy, was cleaning his attic when he found an old sack of seeds he could not identify. He was sure the bag had been left by his late great-grandfather, who had started the farmstead around 1915. Grossi had no idea how long the seeds had sat there, deprived of water, soil and sun, according to Smithsonian Magazine's account of the discovery.[S18]

He planted them anyway. What came up was a wheat unlike the varieties he knew. All the wheat types Grossi had previously seen grew a single ear per plant; this one produced more. "I thought it was a freak of nature," he said, adding that he could not find a mention of it in agricultural books. Searching older references later, he connected the grain to something ancient, and the seed bag to a simpler explanation: his great-grandfather had saved heritage grain, and it survived in the attic because nobody threw it out.[S18]

The wheat became known as grano del miracolo, or "miracle grain." The name carries a biblical echo, and the Smithsonian article frames the discovery that way, but the agricultural story is more concrete. Compared with commercial wheat, the dough did not rise well, Grossi found. As a loaf sat in the oven, though, it filled the kitchen with what he described as a surprisingly nutty aroma. "You get these notes of sweetness that you can't find in regular commercial flour," he said.[S18]

Grossi also found practical advantages. The plant was hardier and more self-sufficient than commercial varieties, fared better during droughts and grew so tall that it overshadowed weeds. Those traits matter in a crop where the dominant post-war strategy has been the opposite: shorter plants that put more energy into grain and respond well to fertilizer and irrigation.[S18]

The midcentury Green Revolution, launched by the American agronomist Norman Borlaug, focused on selective breeding and boosted crop production worldwide, feeding many people. Italy's own wheat sector moved toward higher-yield commercial varieties, and older grains fell out of use. The Smithsonian account notes that grano del miracolo had been grown in Italy until that shift, which explains how a bag of it ended up stored on a family farm.[S18]

Grossi's timing intersected with a broader cultural turn. In Italy in the 1990s, a burgeoning slow-food movement aimed to preserve traditional farming practices and promote natural diets. Grossi hoped miracolo could ride that wave, becoming both a portal to the past and a passage to a future. He also thought it might help other farmers bring back ancient grains.[S18]

The scientific case got a boost from food scientists who analyzed the grain. Compared with modern wheats, grano del miracolo was richer in phosphorus and iron. "The richness of microelements, minerals, and the greater presence of proteins other than gluten were very pleasant surprises," said Perobelli, now a corporate social responsibility and sustainability manager with Rigoni di Asiago, an Italian company that produces organic fruit spreads and honey. The finding is notable because modern breeding has often prioritized yield and baking performance over micronutrient density, though the comparison here is between specific varieties rather than a general verdict on old versus new wheat.[S18]

Commercial progress followed. Over the next few years, Grossi increased production, and Molino Grassi, an Italian milling company, sold the flour to restaurants and consumers in Italy and abroad. That moved miracolo from an attic curiosity into a small supply chain with a named miller, a flour product and paying customers.[S18]

The grain is not without drawbacks. Grossi found that miracolo's height can be a liability, which may be why it fell out of favor during Mussolini's era, when agricultural policy pushed toward higher-yielding varieties. Tall wheat is more prone to lodging, or falling over, especially in wind and heavy rain. Grossi circumvented the problem by planting miracolo with shorter wheat varieties that provide structural support, a mixed-cropping approach that lets the tall plants lean on their neighbors.[S18]

The drought question is where the story becomes more than nostalgia. A farmer based in Sicily told Smithsonian that 2024 brought "one of the worst droughts in the last 35 to 40 years," so severe that many commercial wheat growers abandoned their fields in April, realizing they would not be able to reap the seeds they had sown. That account, from a grower in a different region, points to the conditions under which hardy, deep-rooted or tall-statured heritage grains might attract renewed interest.[S18]

It would be a mistake to treat one attic discovery as a solution to Mediterranean drought. The evidence here is a single farmer's experience, one nutritional analysis and a small commercial operation. There is no published multi-year trial in the supplied material comparing miracolo's yield, water use or disease resistance with modern varieties across regions. Nor is there data on how it performs under the intensive management that most commercial wheat receives. The claim that it is hardier and more drought-tolerant rests on Grossi's observation and the Sicilian grower's framing, not on controlled experiments described in the source.[S18]

What the story does show is a mechanism by which genetic diversity re-enters agriculture. Heritage seeds stored privately, outside formal genebanks, can preserve traits that breeding programs discarded when yield was the overriding goal. A farmer with a sack in an attic is an informal conservation system, and it worked here because the seeds remained viable and someone recognized the plant was unusual. That is a fragile chain: the same bag could easily have been burned or thrown away.[S18]

The economics are equally modest. A specialty flour sold to restaurants and consumers abroad can support a niche, but it does not by itself change what most people eat. Grossi's hope that miracolo could lead a movement is a hope, not an outcome documented in the source. The slow-food market rewards story and provenance, which helps a grain with a biblical name and a family history; it also limits volume, because the price premium depends on scarcity and narrative.[S18]

For bakers, the trade-off is real. A flour that does not rise well is harder to use in standard bread production, even if it tastes distinctive. Its value is more likely in blends, specialty loaves and products where flavor and mineral content justify adaptation by the baker. For farmers, the trade-off is height and lodging risk against drought resilience and weed suppression. For plant breeders, the interesting question is whether the traits Grossi observed can be identified and moved into higher-yielding backgrounds without losing the hardiness.[S18]

The Smithsonian article's biblical framing refers to the story of Gideon, the judge and military leader who recognizes a dream as a sign that God will deliver the Midianites into Israel's hands. The comparison is literary, not agronomic. The useful version of the story is smaller and more testable: an old grain, saved by accident, turned out to have nutritional and agronomic properties that a farmer could sell, and a drought-stressed Mediterranean is a reason to ask what else is sitting in attics and genebanks.[S18]

Grossi's own summary is modest. "I'm quite happy because I developed something that's like a discovery," he said. The next stage is less about wonder and more about measurement: whether miracolo's apparent advantages hold up across seasons, soils and rainfall, and whether a niche flour can become a durable market rather than a curiosity.[S18]

Why it matters

Wheat breeding since the Green Revolution has optimized for short stature, fertilizer response and yield, which helped feed billions but narrowed the genetic base of a staple crop. A farmer reviving a tall, mineral-rich heritage grain shows how privately stored seed can reintroduce traits that formal programs discarded. The immediate beneficiaries are niche millers, specialty bakers and farmers in dry areas looking for hardier plants; the broader relevance is to seed conservation and to how agriculture prices drought tolerance against yield.

What happens next

Expect the miracolo story to be cited in heritage-grain and slow-food marketing, and watch whether millers or universities publish comparative trials on yield, water use and lodging. Without such data, the grain is likely to remain a premium specialty flour rather than a mainstream crop. If Mediterranean droughts intensify, demand for drought-tolerant varieties could rise, but adoption will depend on whether bakers can work with a flour that rises poorly and whether farmers can manage tall plants at scale.
