AI writing detectors flagged a 20-year professional. She lost the book proposal.

NPR reports that detection tools can disagree sharply on the same text, and that writers are now changing how they write to avoid being accused.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

A nonfiction writer with more than 20 years of professional experience submitted writing samples for a book proposal and got back an email saying several artificial intelligence detection tools had flagged her work as machine-generated. She had not used AI to produce the text, she told NPR, and she believes the detector results cost her the chance to write the full proposal.[S16]

NPR identifies the writer as McGraw. She was paid for the samples, she said, but was angry that her work had been dismissed as machine output. The client confirmed to NPR that she had screened McGraw's writing for AI. McGraw described her reaction as a physical one, saying her ears burned with anger.[S16]

The case sits inside a broader problem in publishing and education, where software is increasingly used to judge whether text came from a person or a machine. NPR reports that researchers have found different AI detection tools can reach dramatically different conclusions about the same piece of writing. That variance matters because the tools are being used as gatekeepers, not merely as advisory checks.[S16]

One measure of the pressure comes from a science fiction magazine. Clarke, its publisher, told NPR that submissions doubled by February 2023, after AI agents such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini became widely available, forcing the magazine to temporarily close its submission portal. He estimates his own workload has risen by about 30%, and he said the flood of AI-assisted submissions has introduced mistrust into the process.[S16]

Clarke said he does not personally use AI detectors to screen incoming manuscripts, but he thinks certain detection tools could help overwhelmed editors manage volume, provided they check the detector's findings. He cautioned against turning suspicion into accusation, arguing that a submission should end in a rejection rather than a charge against the writer.[S16]

NPR's reporting includes a test of four detectors on 40 papers. Pangram was the most consistently accurate among the four, correctly identifying AI use in 37 of the 40 papers with combined text and in 37 of the 40 where a human rewrote AI-generated text. Pangram CEO Max Spero said in an emailed statement that his company's detector can make mistakes, particularly when there is not much writing to evaluate.[S16]

The mechanics of detection help explain the errors. Chris Callison-Burch, a professor of computer and information science at the University of Pennsylvania, told NPR that many AI detectors work by asking a large language model how predictable a passage is. Previously published writing can pose an additional problem, he said. An unpublished draft like McGraw's sample would not face that particular issue, but could still be falsely flagged based on patterns in the writer's style.[S16]

NPR reported one detector's output as a 98.7% confidence that a passage was machine-generated, a figure that shows how precise the tools can sound even when their judgments are contested.[S16]

The Authors Guild, which advocates for writers' rights, said in May that the use of AI detectors has created an atmosphere of suspicion, with writers worried about false accusations and about how to defend themselves.[S16]

McGraw said the fear of another accusation has changed how she writes. She now avoids certain words and sentence constructions and occasionally leaves out commas she used to include, hoping the inconsistency will help show that a human wrote the text. She also said it is widely understood that these screeners are not fully accurate.[S16]

Teachers are using software to check whether students used AI, NPR notes, extending the same accuracy questions into classrooms where a false positive can carry academic consequences.[S16]

The practical difficulty for anyone accused is that detection output rarely arrives with an explanation a writer can rebut. A confidence score is not evidence of authorship, and the tools' disagreement with one another gives accused writers little clear ground to stand on. Publishers and editors facing rising submission volumes have reason to want filters, but the NPR reporting shows the filters can misread experienced human prose and that at least one detector's own chief executive acknowledges limits on short samples.[S16]

For now, the burden is falling on writers to prove a negative, and some are pre-emptively altering their style to look less machine-like.[S16]

Why it matters

Detection tools are being used as gatekeepers in publishing and education, so their error rate is not abstract. A false positive can cost a writer paid work or a student an academic record, and the accused often have no way to audit the score. The NPR reporting shows the tools disagree with each other and that at least one vendor acknowledges mistakes on short samples, which shifts risk onto people who did nothing wrong. Writers are already changing word choice and punctuation to avoid suspicion, a behavioral cost that does not show up in accuracy statistics.

What happens next

Expect more institutions to adopt detectors as submission volumes rise, and expect pushback from writers' groups and possibly from institutions facing appeals. Vendors may respond with documentation features or human-review guidance, though nothing in the reporting confirms such plans. The near-term pattern is likely to be uneven: some editors use detectors as a first filter with manual checks, while others avoid them. Legal or contractual challenges are possible but not established.

What to watch

  • Any policy statement from the Authors Guild or similar groups beyond its May comments on detectors
  • Whether Pangram or other vendors publish accuracy data for short samples, the case its CEO cited as error-prone
  • Submission-volume figures or portal closures at magazines and publishers over the next several months
  • Appeals or disputes in education where a student was flagged and contested the result
  • Contract or submission-guideline language requiring writers to disclose AI use or consent to screening
