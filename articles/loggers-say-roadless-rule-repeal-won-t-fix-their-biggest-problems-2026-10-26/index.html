Loggers Say Roadless Rule Repeal Won't Fix Their Biggest Problems

NPR reports that Oregon timber operators face fuel costs, labor shortages and a reduced mill base as the administration pursues a proposed Roadless Rule repeal.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

President Trump is widely expected to soon move to open roughly 44 million acres of national forest, a step he has said will restore timber jobs to struggling small towns and help reduce wildfire risk on overgrown land, according to NPR. A formal repeal of the Roadless Rule is expected early next year. But in timber country around Eugene, Oregon, people who make their living in the forests describe a gap between that promise and the problems they face day to day.[S1]

Sean Smith, chief executive of the family-owned Starfire Lumber in Cottage Grove, Oregon, told NPR that the executive orders behind the push are not useless because they set a tone, but that the industry has more pressing challenges now. Standing in a busy lumber yard with the sawmill running behind stacks of freshly cut timber, Smith pointed first to high diesel prices and inflation raising operating costs. Smith does support the reversal, saying it would eventually give the industry a more reliable local timber supply. He also argues that much of the nearby Willamette National Forest, roaded or not, is dangerously overgrown, and that accumulated fuel makes those forests more prone to catastrophic wildfire.[S1]

The administration, the timber industry and some local officials are pressing for repeal on the argument that it would give the Forest Service more flexibility to carry out wildfire prevention work, including tree thinning, on land that has largely functioned as wilderness since 2001.[S1]

Under recent Republican and Democratic administrations alike, the Forest Service has struggled to get projects going even on forests that already have roads. Lawsuits and red tape remain ongoing challenges, and so does finding enough workers. Jeff Brink, a second-generation logger near Eugene, described the difficulty of recruiting log-truck drivers for very early starts, saying a fast-food employer offered comparable pay. For many smaller, less established loggers who are already struggling, Brink said, labor shortages and high diesel prices could be the tipping point.[S1]

The scale of the industry's contraction helps explain that caution. Timber sales on U.S. federal land have dropped by close to 90 percent since the 1990s, a decline NPR attributes to tightening environmental laws and other market forces including cheaper Canadian lumber imports. Brink said the infrastructure simply is not there for the forest work that needs doing, let alone for a promised timber boom. Since the 1990s, an estimated 400 mills across the broader Northwest have closed, amounting to the loss of about 35,000 jobs, and seven mills have closed in Oregon since 2024 alone.[S1]

Industry experts project that overturning the Roadless Rule could produce a five to ten percent boost in federal timber sales, according to NPR. NPR contrasts that projection with the decline in federal sales since the 1990s. It is an estimate about a possible future increase, rather than an increase already recorded.[S1]

Opposition is already forming. Several national environmental groups have signaled they plan to sue once the administration formally repeals the rule, which is expected by early next year.[S1]

Timothy Ingalsbee, a retired Forest Service firefighter who runs a national firefighter safety and advocacy group, offered a different view in the NPR report. Ingalsbee warned that more roads could increase human-caused wildfires; NPR describes such fires as having risen over several decades. He disputed the administration's stated rationale of preventing wildfire and creating logging work.[S1]

NPR also describes work that is already sustaining a logging business independently of the proposed repeal. Brink's father and uncles started Brink Land Development in 1981. Brink said his company is getting by through a steady stream of forest restoration projects on private land, along with recent salvage timber sales in areas burned by wildfire. That account distinguishes the company's current workload from the larger increase in federal timber sales being promised. Brink described the industry as much smaller than it had been when his relatives started the company.[S1]

At Starfire Lumber, Smith also described the difficulty of operating under federal forest and timber policies that shift from one presidential administration to another. He told NPR that the changes create a hard environment for keeping a business going. His support for access to a more reliable local timber supply therefore sits alongside concern about day-to-day costs and the policy setting in which a mill operates.[S1]

Why it matters

The Roadless Rule repeal is being framed as a fix for timber jobs and wildfire risk, but the operators NPR interviewed say fuel costs, inflation, labor shortages, litigation and a shrunken mill base are the binding constraints. If the policy delivers only a modest sales increase, it may not change conditions in timber towns, while still triggering litigation and a fight over road building in national forests.

What happens next

A formal repeal is expected early next year, followed by likely legal challenges from environmental groups. In the meantime, diesel prices, inflation and hiring conditions will shape whether small loggers survive long enough to bid on any additional sales.

What to watch

  • Formal publication of the Roadless Rule repeal and its effective date
  • Whether national environmental groups file suit and how quickly a court acts
  • Diesel prices and inflation readings that Smith and Brink cite as immediate pressures
  • Forest Service project pipelines and staffing on already-roaded forests
  • Further mill closures or openings in Oregon and the broader Northwest
