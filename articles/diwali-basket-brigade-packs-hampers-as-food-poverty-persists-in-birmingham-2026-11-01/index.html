Diwali Basket Brigade Packs Hampers as Food Poverty Persists in Birmingham

About 300 volunteers gathered in Hockley to assemble food parcels for households facing the rising cost of living, as The Diwali Foundation targets 6,000 hampers this year.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

About 300 volunteers gathered at the RSRS Centre in Hockley, Birmingham, on the weekend before Diwali to pack food parcels for households struggling with the rising cost of living, the BBC reported. The event was organised by The Diwali Foundation, a Herefordshire-based charity, and marked the ninth year it has brought together volunteers from a range of backgrounds to assemble hampers ahead of the religious festival.[S7]

The parcels were taken from Hockley to residents living in homes provided by Walsall Housing Group (whg), a housing association. A London event was also held on Saturday, with similar gatherings planned in Manchester and Leicester for Sunday, according to the BBC. Organisers have set a target of 6,000 food hampers this year, which would exceed a 2025 record in which more than 80,000 meals were provided.[S7]

The Diwali Foundation said more than 12% of UK households are experiencing food poverty, a figure it cited as the backdrop to the packing effort. The charity works with about 40 different food banks and charities to reach vulnerable households, and says it has provided around 23,470 food hampers to those in need to date.[S7]

The event's scale reflects both the reach of the charity's network and the persistence of demand. A representative of Walsall Housing Group, quoted by the BBC, said: "Our customers are having a really hard time," adding that "the cost of living is not easing." The comments point to the pressure on households in social housing, where fixed or slowly rising incomes meet food, energy and other essential costs.[S7]

The Diwali Basket Brigade is timed to the festival, which celebrates light, renewal and community. Volunteers from different faiths and backgrounds joined the packing effort, a feature the charity has emphasised across its nine-year run. The hampers are not a substitute for ongoing food bank provision; they are a seasonal contribution routed through a network of local partners, including housing providers and food charities.[S7]

The 6,000-hamper target for this year is a measure of ambition rather than a confirmed outcome. The charity's 2025 record of more than 80,000 meals provided gives a sense of the volume involved, though the relationship between meals and hampers is not specified in the reporting. Whether the target is met will depend on volunteer turnout, donations and the logistics of distribution across Birmingham, London, Manchester and Leicester.[S7]

The broader context is a UK food poverty picture that charities say has not improved. The Diwali Foundation's 12% figure is its own characterisation of household food poverty, and the BBC report does not attribute it to official statistics. Independent measures of food insecurity vary by methodology and survey period, so the figure should be read as the charity's working estimate rather than a definitive count.[S7]

What is clear from the reporting is the operational model: a central packing event, a network of roughly 40 partner organisations, and distribution through housing associations such as whg. That model allows the charity to reach households that may not use traditional food banks, though it also means the charity depends on partners to identify recipients and on volunteers to assemble and move parcels.[S7]

The timing matters for the households involved. Diwali falls in the autumn, when energy costs typically rise and budgets tighten ahead of winter. A hamper provides short-term relief, but the charity's own framing — a ninth consecutive year, a rising target, a partner describing customers as "having a really hard time" — suggests the need is structural rather than seasonal.[S7]

For Birmingham, the event is one of several community responses to cost-of-living pressure. The city's voluntary sector has expanded food provision in recent years, and the Diwali Basket Brigade adds a faith-linked dimension that organisers say brings together volunteers who might not otherwise work on the same project. The BBC report does not quantify how many households will receive parcels from the Hockley event specifically, nor how the 6,000 target is divided across cities.[S7]

Uncertainties remain. The charity's 23,470 cumulative hampers figure is not broken down by year. The 2025 record of more than 80,000 meals is not directly comparable to a hamper count. And the 12% food poverty estimate is the charity's own. These gaps matter for anyone trying to assess whether the 2026 target represents growth in capacity or growth in need.[S7]

What the reporting does establish is a concrete event: hundreds of volunteers, a Birmingham community centre, parcels moving to housing association residents, and a charity planning similar events in three other English cities. The Diwali Foundation's target of 6,000 hampers sets a benchmark that can be checked once the festival season ends.[S7]

Why it matters

Food poverty affects household health, school attendance and local service demand, and the Diwali Basket Brigade shows how faith-based charities are filling gaps that statutory provision and mainstream food banks do not fully cover. The event also tests whether a volunteer-and-partner model can scale across four cities in a single weekend, which matters for how seasonal relief is organised in the UK.

What happens next

The charity will distribute parcels through its roughly 40 partner organisations and housing providers, with events in Manchester and Leicester following the London and Birmingham gatherings. Whether the 6,000-hamper target is met will become clearer after the festival season, when the charity is likely to report totals. Demand from households in social housing is unlikely to ease quickly if cost-of-living pressure persists.
