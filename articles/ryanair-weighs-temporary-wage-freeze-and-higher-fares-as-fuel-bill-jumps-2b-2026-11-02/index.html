Ryanair weighs temporary wage freeze and higher fares as fuel bill jumps €2bn

The airline says pre-tax profit fell 16% in the six months to September and that fuel costs will stay elevated "well into 2027" unless the Iran conflict is resolved quickly.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Ryanair is considering a temporary wage freeze for staff and expects European airfares to rise, as it absorbs a fuel bill it forecasts will be about €2bn higher in the short term. The airline tied the increase to a sharp rise in fuel costs following the US-Israel war with Iran, and said it expects fuel prices to remain high "well into 2027" unless there is a "swift resolution" to the conflict.[S1]

The disclosure came alongside half-year results showing pre-tax profits fell 16% to €2.4bn in the six months to the end of September. Ryanair attributed much of the decline to higher fuel costs, which it said jumped by 15% over the period. The carrier described the possible pay freeze as a temporary measure to help cover the gap, and said it expects fares across the European market to increase to help recover the cost.[S1]

The fuel mechanics explain why a relatively contained conflict has produced such a large number. Ryanair said 80% of its fuel bill had been hedged — bought in advance at fixed prices — but the cost of the remaining 20% had doubled. Hedging shields an airline from spot-price swings on the covered portion of its consumption; it does not remove exposure on the unhedged share, and it can also lock in prices that later look expensive if markets fall. For a carrier of Ryanair's scale, a doubling on even a fifth of the bill is a large absolute sum, which is why the company translates it into a multi-billion-euro forecast increase rather than a marginal cost line.[S1]

The fare signal is the part that reaches passengers. Chief executive Michael O'Leary said last month that he expects the firm and other airlines to raise fares by 15% to 20% next year. That is a forecast from the company's most senior executive, not a confirmed pricing decision, and it describes the wider European market rather than Ryanair alone. It nonetheless points to a second channel through which the fuel shock could reach consumers: if carriers pass through higher costs, ticket prices rise; if they compete on price instead, margins absorb the hit.[S1]

The labour element is less settled. Ryanair said it is considering a temporary wage freeze, but the reporting does not specify which staff groups would be affected, how long a freeze would last, whether it would apply across the group's bases, or what consultation would precede it. Those details will determine how the proposal lands with unions and crew, and whether "temporary" survives contact with a fuel outlook the company itself extends into 2027. A freeze that covers pilots, cabin crew and ground staff would be a broader industrial-relations event than one confined to a single function or country; the absence of that detail is a real gap, not a formality.[S1]

The results also set a baseline for how much room Ryanair has to absorb costs without deeper cuts. A €2.4bn pre-tax profit for the half year is a decline, but it is still a profit, and the hedge book means most of its fuel is already priced. The pressure is concentrated in the unhedged remainder and in the period beyond the hedge book's coverage. That is why the company's language ties the outlook to the duration of the Iran conflict: a shorter war would ease spot prices and weaken the case for both the wage freeze and aggressive fare increases; a longer one would push the problem into the next financial year, when the hedge position may look different.[S1]

For passengers, the practical question is timing. O'Leary's 15% to 20% figure refers to next year, and Ryanair's statement that it expects European fares to rise is an expectation about the market, not a schedule of specific ticket increases. Travellers booking into 2027 should treat the guidance as a direction of travel rather than a confirmed price list. For staff, the open questions are scope and duration. For investors, the key variables are the path of fuel prices, the size of the unhedged exposure in coming reporting periods, and whether rivals match fare increases or compete on price and squeeze margins.[S1]

The wider context is that fuel is one of the few costs an airline cannot easily substitute away from in the short run. Carriers can hedge, adjust capacity, retire older aircraft or slow hiring, but they cannot change what their fleet burns. When a geopolitical shock moves oil, the effect shows up first in the unhedged portion of the bill and then in pricing and labour decisions. Ryanair's half-year numbers and its stated options — a possible wage freeze and expected fare increases — are an early, concrete example of that chain running through a major European carrier.[S1]

What the reporting does not establish is equally important. There is no confirmed date for a wage-freeze decision, no figure for how much the freeze would save, no breakdown of which routes or markets would see fare increases, and no independent confirmation that other airlines will follow O'Leary's 15% to 20% expectation. Those gaps separate a company signalling its intentions from a market-wide pricing shift that passengers would actually feel. The distinction matters because Ryanair's cost base and its low-fare model make it unusually sensitive to fuel: a carrier that competes on price has less room than a premium airline to absorb a cost shock without changing either fares or pay.[S1]

Ryanair's own framing places the conflict at the centre of its outlook. That is a testable claim: if fuel prices ease as the conflict de-escalates, the case for a wage freeze weakens and the fare guidance may soften. If the conflict persists, the company has already signalled where it will look for savings — staff costs — and where it expects to recover revenue — ticket prices. The next reporting period will show whether those signals become decisions.[S1]

Why it matters

Ryanair is one of Europe's largest low-cost carriers, so its cost response is a test case for how the sector handles a fuel shock. A wage freeze shifts part of the fuel bill onto staff; expected fare increases shift part onto passengers. If other carriers follow, the effect reaches household travel budgets and airline labour relations across Europe, not just Ryanair's balance sheet.

What happens next

Watch whether Ryanair converts the "considering" language into a formal wage-freeze proposal, and whether it publishes route-level or market-level fare guidance beyond O'Leary's 15% to 20% forecast. The fuel outlook depends heavily on the Iran conflict; a swift resolution would weaken the case for both measures, while a prolonged one would push pressure into the next financial year.

What to watch

  • Any Ryanair statement or filing confirming a wage freeze, with scope, duration and expected savings
  • Ryanair's next quarterly results and the size of its unhedged fuel exposure
  • European fare data for 2027 bookings versus O'Leary's 15% to 20% forecast
  • Statements from rival carriers such as easyJet and Wizz Air on fare and capacity plans
  • Union or crew responses to the wage-freeze proposal
