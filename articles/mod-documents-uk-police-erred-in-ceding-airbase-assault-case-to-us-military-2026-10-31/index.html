MoD Documents: UK Police Erred in Ceding Airbase Assault Case to US Military

Internal ministry documents say Cambridgeshire police should not have handed the investigation of a US fighter pilot to American military authorities, as a cross-government inquiry examines how serious crimes involving US personnel end up outside British courts.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Ministry of Defence officials have privately concluded that Cambridgeshire police should not have allowed the US military to take control of the investigation into an American fighter pilot accused of assaulting a British woman, according to documents obtained by the Guardian and published on 31 October. The officials said the force should “absolutely” not have surrendered the case, describing the handover as a clear breach of a long-established agreement governing how the two countries handle alleged crimes by US personnel stationed in Britain.[S15]

The case centres on Capt Jacob Wulfson, who was accused of sexually assaulting Sarah Steele in his Cambridge flat in December 2023 after the pair met on the Tinder dating app. Rather than face an English criminal court, Wulfson was prosecuted in a military tribunal on a US airbase in neighbouring Suffolk. The Guardian reports that all the lawyers involved in that proceeding were American.[S15]

The documents add a formal, internal dimension to a dispute that became public in June, when the Guardian first disclosed the case. Downing Street responded at the time by calling it “deeply distressing” and “very concerning” that UK authorities had not been involved in prosecuting Wulfson, and the government announced a high-level inquiry into how and why police forces have been transferring investigations into serious crimes to US military police. The Ministry of Defence, which manages the military relationship with Washington, is one of the key departments taking part, alongside the Home Office, Ministry of Justice and Foreign Office.[S15]

The jurisdictional question at the heart of the case is not ambiguous on paper. Under the terms of the UK-US agreement, police in Britain have the primary right to investigate alleged crimes by Americans stationed in the country. The MoD officials' assessment, as reported, is that Cambridgeshire police gave up that right when they let US military police quickly assume control of the Wulfson investigation. The practical effect was that a British complainant's allegation was adjudicated inside a US military system, on a US airbase, rather than through the Crown Prosecution Service and the English courts.[S15]

Cambridgeshire police have not accepted the characterisation directly. The force said it is conducting a review of the case with what it called “the assistance of material provided” by US Air Force lawyers, and that it “remained engaged with the victim.” It added that it is “working with government, wider policing and criminal justice partners to ensure greater clarity of jurisdictional decision making.” That language points to a process problem — unclear decision-making at the point where a local force decides whether to assert or waive its primacy — rather than to a single disputed judgement.[S15]

The Wulfson case is not the only one feeding the inquiry. The Guardian's reporting references Tyrion Davis, who allegedly raped two women in Suffolk. He was convicted of one charge of sexual assault but cleared of 10 further counts of sexual assault and abusive sexual contact. The details of how that case was handled, and by whom, are not fully set out in the available material, but its inclusion signals that the inquiry is looking at a pattern rather than an isolated incident.[S15]

The scale of the underlying relationship helps explain why the issue has become politically sensitive. The US has more than 12,000 military personnel in the UK, stationed at at least 15 bases and facilities. Those personnel live and work in British communities, and allegations involving them can arise in ordinary civilian settings — a flat in Cambridge, a town in Suffolk — where local police are the first responders. The agreement that governs those encounters is meant to balance two interests: the US military's desire to maintain discipline and prosecute its own people, and the UK's interest in ensuring that crimes committed on British soil are investigated and tried under British law.[S15]

The US military, according to the reporting, aims to prosecute as many of its personnel as possible within the boundaries of its agreement with the UK. That is an institutional incentive, not necessarily a legal entitlement, and the boundary is precisely what is in dispute. Where US authorities assert jurisdiction and UK police acquiesce, the agreement's primacy provision becomes effectively unenforced. The MoD documents suggest that at least some officials inside the department responsible for the relationship believe that is what happened in the Wulfson case.[S15]

The political timeline matters. The case became public in June, prompting criticism from Downing Street and the establishment of the cross-government inquiry. The inquiry's remit is broad: it is examining how US military personnel are not being brought to justice in British courts. That framing implies the government accepts there is a systemic issue, even as individual forces and the MoD may disagree about where responsibility lies in any given case. The MoD documents reported on 31 October sharpen that question by putting a departmental judgement on record: the force, in the officials' view, got it wrong.[S15]

For Sarah Steele, the practical consequences are already settled in one sense — the prosecution happened in a US tribunal, not an English court, and no British prosecution now looms. What the inquiry can change is the handling of future cases. The variables it can influence are procedural: whether police forces receive clearer guidance on when they must assert primacy; whether the MoD, Home Office and Crown Prosecution Service establish a notification or review mechanism before a case is handed to US military police; and whether victims are told, in plain terms, which legal system will handle their allegation and what rights they have in it.[S15]

There are limits to what the public record currently establishes. The documents obtained by the Guardian are described in the reporting, but their full text, the identity of the officials quoted, and the precise legal reasoning behind the “clear breach” assessment are not set out in the available material. It is also not established whether the MoD's view has been formally conveyed to Cambridgeshire police, whether the force disputes it, or whether the inquiry has reached interim conclusions. The US Air Force's position on the handover is not detailed beyond the general statement that the US military seeks to prosecute its personnel within the agreement's boundaries.[S15]

The case also sits inside a wider question about accountability that predates it. Status-of-forces arrangements are common among allied militaries, and they routinely allocate investigative and prosecutorial responsibility. Disputes tend to arise not over the text but over early-stage practice — who arrives first, who controls evidence, who interviews the complainant, and who decides that a case is “theirs.” Those decisions are often made by officers on the ground under time pressure, which is why the inquiry's focus on “jurisdictional decision making” is likely to produce recommendations about training and escalation rather than about the treaty itself.[S15]

For the government, the political risk is twofold. If the inquiry concludes that police forces routinely ceded cases they should have kept, ministers will face questions about why guidance was not clearer and why oversight was absent. If it concludes that the Wulfson handover was an isolated error, the broader criticism — that British victims of US personnel can end up outside British courts — will not be fully answered. The MoD documents push the evidence toward the first conclusion, at least for this case.[S15]

What is not in doubt is the sequence. A woman reported an assault in Cambridge in December 2023. US military police took control of the investigation. A US military tribunal on a Suffolk airbase tried the case. British police did not prosecute. In October, MoD officials said, according to the documents, that they should not have let that happen. The inquiry now has to decide what changes.[S15]

Why it matters

The case tests whether the UK's primacy right under its status-of-forces agreement with the US is real in practice. If local police can hand serious cases to US military police without challenge, British victims' access to English courts depends on decisions made by officers under time pressure, with little visible oversight. The MoD's internal judgement shifts the question from a single disputed case to whether guidance, notification and review mechanisms are adequate across all forces hosting US personnel.

What happens next

The cross-government inquiry, involving the MoD, Home Office, Ministry of Justice and Foreign Office, is expected to examine how cases are transferred and may recommend clearer rules on asserting primacy. Cambridgeshire police's review, conducted with material from US Air Force lawyers, could produce its own findings. The political pressure point is whether ministers publish inquiry conclusions and whether guidance to forces changes before the next high-profile case arises.

What to watch

  • Publication of the cross-government inquiry's terms of reference, interim findings or final report
  • Any revised Home Office or National Police Chiefs' Council guidance on asserting primacy in cases involving US personnel
  • Cambridgeshire police's review outcome and whether it accepts or disputes the MoD's assessment
  • Statements from the US Air Force or Embassy about how jurisdiction is asserted in UK cases
  • Parliamentary questions or select committee evidence sessions on the number of cases transferred to US military authorities
