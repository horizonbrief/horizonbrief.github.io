Healey threatens veto on offshore wind projects that do not cut bills

The chancellor says ministers will only sign off new subsidy contracts if the grid can absorb the power without raising consumer costs, as a £3bn energy budget cut sharpens questions about Labour's clean power target.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

John Healey has said he will veto new offshore wind projects if they will not lower bills, a position that adds to concerns the government is stepping back from its pro-renewables agenda. Under the approach he described, new offshore wind projects bidding for subsidy contracts would only be signed off by ministers if they believe all the power generated could be carried by Britain's electricity grid without pushing up costs for consumers.[S12]

The condition targets a specific cost problem. The National Energy System Operator (Neso), which is funded through energy bills, must pay wind turbines to switch off when there is not enough cabling capacity to move their power to consumers. Those constraint payments show up on bills. A National Audit Office report in September warned that homes and businesses could face a potential annual bill of £7.8bn by 2030 to deal with the issue.[S12]

The veto threat lands alongside budget decisions that have already unsettled parts of the government's energy programme. Healey revealed more than £3bn worth of cuts to the energy department's budget, much of which will pay for higher defence spending. He announced on Wednesday that the government would fund the defence investment plan in part through cuts to other departments. The Guardian reported that the combination appears to be a further shift away from Labour's target of having clean power by 2030.[S12]

Allies of Ed Miliband and Fahnbulleh insisted the government's green agenda remained as strong as ever. They pointed to Healey's announcement of a long-term move to shift green costs away from bills and onto taxpayers, something the two energy secretaries have both championed. The government also has a target to raise a scheme's uptake to 250,000 a year by 2030 and was planning to increase its funding from £400m this year to just over £700m by 2029-30.[S12]

The immediate question is how the test would work in practice. Ministers would be judging whether a project's full output could be accommodated without raising consumer costs, a standard that depends on grid capacity that is not yet built and on how constraint costs are forecast. The government has not set out, in the reporting available, the threshold it would apply or which projects might fail it.[S12]

Why it matters

The veto threat shifts risk onto offshore wind developers and their supply chains. A project that wins a subsidy contract but cannot show its output will be absorbed without raising bills now faces ministerial rejection, which changes how developers size and site schemes and how they model grid connection dates. For households, the policy is aimed at the constraint payments that Neso currently passes through to bills, with the NAO putting a potential £7.8bn annual cost by 2030. For the government, the £3bn energy budget cut and the bill-focused test put two priorities in tension: cutting consumer costs and keeping the pace needed for clean power by 2030.

What happens next

Ministers are likely to face early tests when the next round of subsidy contract decisions reaches sign-off, and developers will be watching whether the grid-capacity condition is applied strictly or used as leverage to renegotiate. The clean power 2030 target is already under pressure from the budget cuts, so the next budget and energy department spending plans will show whether the shift is rhetorical or structural. Expect more detail on how constraint costs are forecast and who bears them.

What to watch

  • The first subsidy contract decision after this statement, and whether any bid is rejected on bill-impact grounds
  • Energy department spending plans following the £3bn cut, and whether clean power 2030 milestones slip
  • Neso forecasts for constraint payments and any revision to the NAO's £7.8bn by 2030 estimate
  • Grid connection and cabling capacity announcements for offshore wind zones
  • Whether the shift of green costs from bills to taxpayers appears in legislation or budget documents
