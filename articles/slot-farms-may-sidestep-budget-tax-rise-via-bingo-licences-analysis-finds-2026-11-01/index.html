Slot farms may sidestep budget tax rise via bingo licences, analysis finds

Guardian analysis suggests venues run by Merkur and Admiral could escape a business rates change aimed at adult gaming centres, as ministers face pressure to close a licensing gap.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

High street "slot farms" operated by the UK's two largest companies in the sector may avoid a budget tax increase because the venues hold bingo licences, according to Guardian analysis published on 1 November. The finding lands on a policy the government has presented as a way to clean up high streets and raise revenue from adult gaming centres, known as AGCs.[S17]

The budget measure has two parts. It raises duty on slot machines, a change that will also affect bookmakers and casinos, and it removes a business rates discount aimed at leisure and hospitality businesses from AGCs and vape shops in England. The prime minister, Andy Burnham, has been a vocal critic of AGCs and posted on Instagram after the budget to support the policy, which the Guardian describes as a key plank of his pitch to improve British high streets.[S17]

The complication is licensing. Many shops owned by Merkur and Admiral have obtained bingo licences for venues that appear to offer slot machines almost exclusively, the Guardian reports. If those venues are treated as bingo halls rather than AGCs for business rates purposes, the budget's removal of the discount may not reach them. Operators could also convert their remaining AGC licences to bingo, the paper adds, which would widen the potential gap if councils and the Treasury do not draw a firm line.[S17]

The Guardian's report does not put a figure on how many venues hold bingo licences, how much revenue could be at stake, or how the two companies would respond to the budget. Those gaps matter because the scale of any avoidance determines whether the measure raises the sums intended or mainly reshapes how operators label their premises.[S17]

The mechanism turns on definitions rather than on the machines themselves. A business rates discount is applied according to how a property is classified and used, and a bingo licence is one input into that judgment. Paul Turner-Mitchell, a business rates expert at the real estate intelligence company Altus Group, told the Guardian that the outcome could hinge on how the Treasury defines an AGC and how local councils choose to enforce the rules. "A bingo licence alone does not settle its business rates treatment," he said. That leaves room for dispute between operators, billing authorities and central government over what a venue actually is when most of its floor space is taken up by slot machines.[S17]

The political pressure is already visible. Alex Ballinger, the Labour MP who co-chairs the gambling reform all-party parliamentary group, welcomed the duty increase on slot machines but urged the government to be vigilant about the loophole. "We must ensure these licences cannot be used to sidestep the new business rates rules," he said. He also called on the Department for Culture, Media and Sport to press ahead with proposals to tighten licensing rules, which are subject to a consultation. "The government has already recognised this problem, and I am confident ministers will work with the regulator to close loopholes and ensure enforcement going forward," he said.[S17]

The Guardian's report also references enforcement history around Merkur. It says the company was fined £100,000 after the Guardian revealed details of Merkur's exploitation of a person named Hughes, who has since died. The report does not provide further detail on that case in the supplied material, and the fine should be read as part of a wider record of regulatory scrutiny rather than as evidence about the bingo-licence question itself.[S17]

For the Treasury, the risk is that a measure announced as a levy on a specific kind of high street venue is tested by the licensing system that sits alongside it. Business rates are collected by local authorities, and councils vary in capacity and appetite for reclassification fights. If some billing authorities treat bingo-licensed premises as bingo halls and others treat them as AGCs, the effective tax burden could differ by postcode, which is the kind of inconsistency that tends to produce appeals and lobbying.[S17]

For operators, the incentive is straightforward. If a bingo licence lowers the rates bill, converting or retaining one becomes a commercial calculation, not only a question of what games are offered. The Guardian's analysis suggests that calculation may already be under way at Merkur and Admiral venues. Neither company's response to the budget is included in the supplied reporting, and it is not established whether they intend to expand conversions or contest the classification.[S17]

For high streets, the question is whether the policy changes the mix of premises or mainly the paperwork attached to them. Burnham's public support for the measure frames AGCs as a problem for town centres. If venues can keep operating largely as before under a different licence, the visible effect on high streets may be smaller than the budget rhetoric implies, even if the duty increase still raises costs for the machines themselves.[S17]

The consultation at DCMS is the other moving part. Ballinger's call for tighter licensing rules points to a process that could close the gap, but consultations do not bind ministers to a timetable. The Guardian reports that the government has recognised the problem; it does not report a committed date for legislation or a decision on whether bingo-licensed venues will be explicitly captured by the rates change.[S17]

What is established is narrower than the headline claim. The budget raises slot machine duty and removes the leisure and hospitality rates discount from AGCs and vape shops in England. Merkur and Admiral venues have obtained bingo licences for premises that appear to offer slot machines almost exclusively, according to Guardian analysis. A bingo licence alone does not determine rates treatment, a specialist told the paper. An MP on the gambling reform group wants the loophole closed and the licensing consultation accelerated. What is not established is how many venues will actually escape the higher bill, how much money is involved, or whether councils will treat the licences as decisive.[S17]

Those unknowns are likely to shape the next phase. If billing authorities begin issuing bills that treat bingo-licensed slot venues as AGCs, operators may appeal, and the resulting cases could clarify the definition faster than ministerial statements. If councils take the licences at face value, the Treasury may need to amend the rules to capture the venues directly. Either path would test whether the budget measure is enforced through rates administration or through a licensing overhaul that has not yet been completed.[S17]
