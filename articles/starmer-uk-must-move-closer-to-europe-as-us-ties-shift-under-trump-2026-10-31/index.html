Starmer: UK must move closer to Europe as US ties shift under Trump

In his first major political intervention since leaving No 10, Keir Starmer said the change in the US relationship is unlikely to reverse after Trump, and that a stronger Europe needs Britain at its heart.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Keir Starmer used his first significant political intervention since leaving Downing Street to argue that the UK and other European nations must draw closer together as the United States becomes a less reliable partner. Speaking at the British Irish Chamber of Commerce on Friday, the former prime minister said a stronger Europe was vital and that the UK had to be at its heart.[S2]

Starmer warned European leaders about the threat posed by Russia, among others, and said the shift in relations with Washington during Donald Trump's presidency was not likely to reset once Trump leaves the Oval Office. He accused European leaders of having been caught out by these new realities twice already. "Things will not go back to the way they were before," he said, according to The Guardian.[S2]

The remarks mark a sharper public position than the one Starmer struck three months earlier, when, amid Trump's war with Iran and his threats to withdraw from Nato, he described Europe as a "partnership for a dangerous world we must navigate together." At that time he said the ties should go beyond defence, that Brexit had done "deep damage" to the UK economy, and that he wanted to be "more ambitious," including pursuing "closer economic cooperation [and] closer security cooperation."[S2]

His successor, Andy Burnham, has also signalled a more pro-European direction. Soon after taking office, Burnham said he intended to be "bolder" about closer ties with the EU, and he has said he wants a public debate on how the UK could move to a closer relationship with the EU after the next election.[S2]

The government's current posture was on display this week at a meeting of European defence ministers in Paris, where Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said they were "united and unwavering" in support for Ukraine in the face of Russian hostility. Streeting described the UK as a leading Nato member pledged to work alongside European allies to guarantee security in Ukraine.[S2]

Starmer's intervention places the argument about the US relationship and Europe's security architecture inside British domestic politics, at a moment when both the former and current prime ministers are pushing in the same general direction. The Guardian reports that Starmer framed the change in Washington's posture as structural rather than tied to one president, a claim that goes further than simply criticising Trump's conduct.[S2]

The evidence available does not detail any new policy proposals from Starmer, nor does it specify what "closer" cooperation would mean in trade, defence procurement or movement of people. Burnham's call for a public debate after the next election suggests the timing of any formal shift remains open.[S2]

Why it matters

The intervention matters most for British domestic politics and for how European capitals read London's reliability. Starmer is arguing that the US shift is structural, not a Trump-specific episode, which if accepted by the current government would raise the political cost of treating Washington as the default anchor and lower the cost of deeper EU alignment on defence and economic cooperation. Burnham's stated intent to be "bolder" and his call for a public debate after the next election give that argument an institutional path, while Streeting's Paris remarks show the government already leaning on European defence coordination for Ukraine.

What happens next

Expect the debate to stay rhetorical in the near term, with Starmer and Burnham testing how far pro-European language can go before concrete commitments are demanded. The next observable steps are likely to be Burnham's framing of the post-election debate, any government language on EU security or economic cooperation, and whether Streeting's Paris coordination produces joint commitments on Ukraine.

What to watch

  • Any formal government statement or paper setting out terms for closer UK-EU security or economic cooperation
  • Burnham's public remarks on the timing and scope of a post-election EU debate
  • Joint outputs from European defence ministers' meetings on Ukraine, including UK commitments
  • Whether Starmer's "will not go back" framing is echoed by current ministers or treated as a personal view
  • UK-US diplomatic and defence contacts that indicate whether Washington treats the shift as structural
