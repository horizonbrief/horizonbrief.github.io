Special Education Teachers Describe Joy, Isolation and a Deepening Shortage

NPR's special education diaries find a field stretched by rising student numbers, heavy paperwork and thin staffing, even as teachers say relationships with students keep them in the work.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Special education in American public schools is under strain from two directions at once, according to NPR reporting published October 26. The number of students who need special education services keeps rising, while the supply of teachers trained and willing to do the job has not kept pace. NPR, drawing on interviews with about a dozen special educators from public schools around the country, describes a workforce that finds deep satisfaction in its students but is worn down by paperwork, isolation and pay that often does not reflect the difficulty of the role.[S1]

The scale of the problem is significant. According to the Learning Policy Institute, an education research organization cited by NPR, 45 of the 50 states report a shortage of special educators. NPR reports that the number receiving special education services now exceeds 8.2 million, roughly one in six public school students, adding to demand for qualified staff. As demand grows, the gap between the number of children who need specialized instruction and the number of qualified adults available to provide it widens.[S1]

NPR's reporting pushes back on a common assumption about why the field struggles to hire. The popular explanation is that the students are too difficult to work with, so teachers avoid the job. Liz Bettini, a former special education teacher who is now a researcher and professor at Boston University, told NPR that this is not what the evidence shows. "That is not the case," she said, adding that the idea has not been borne out in her research at all.[S1]

What the research does point to is a set of working conditions. Special educators can feel cut off from other school staff, and they often carry responsibility for more student needs than one person can reasonably manage. The volume of required paperwork can be overwhelming. These teachers frequently teach across several subjects and grade levels rather than focusing on a single class. Many feel their pay does not match the essential nature of the work.[S1]

The job itself extends well beyond classroom instruction. One study cited by NPR found that special educators spend only about a third of their day on teaching. The rest goes to tracking student goals, coordinating specialized services such as speech therapy or extra reading help, training support staff, and writing individualized education programs, known as IEPs. The plans record students' needs and goals, and preparing them is part of the non-instructional workload described in the NPR account.[S1]

Pay structures add to the pressure. In many states, special education teachers must hold specialized certification and training, but they typically do not earn more for it. According to a recent study cited by NPR, teachers in 32 states receive no extra pay for taking on the hardest-to-staff positions, a category that includes special education. The result is a role with added credential requirements and added workload but, in many places, no added compensation.[S1]

Some states have tried financial incentives. NPR reports that Hawaii added $10,000 to annual special education salaries in 2020 and that vacancies fell by 35 percent, while retention remained a difficulty. The account does not establish that pay alone resolves the staffing problem. Seventeen states now pay teachers additional money to work in hard-to-staff subjects, including special education, down from 22 states four years earlier, according to NPR.[S1]

The pipeline of new teachers is another constraint. Chad Aldeman, an education policy researcher, analyzed federal data a few years ago and found that about 46,000 special education teachers leave public schools each year, while teacher preparation programs train fewer than 30,000 annually to replace them. Aldeman described the figure as a crude estimate, but said the rapid growth in demand for special education teachers is outpacing the supply of people trained for those jobs. Many schools end up hiring teachers who are not fully qualified for the work.[S1]

NPR's interviews also captured what keeps teachers in the field. All 13 special educators NPR spoke with said building relationships with students is their favorite part of the work. At the same time, they described student needs that are more complex than those of many general education students, a reality that shapes both the rewards and the daily difficulty of the job.[S1]

Individual accounts illustrate how working conditions can shift. In Philadelphia, teacher Anderson-Walker told NPR that his biggest struggle was juggling IEP paperwork alongside teaching duties. Last year, his school, Boys' Latin of Philadelphia, hired staff members responsible for the paperwork side of IEPs, freeing teachers for classroom work. "Not having that stress is amazing," he said. In Illinois, first-year teacher Fitzgerald said she felt overwhelmed by her workload at the start of the year, so she worked with school leadership to build more dedicated planning time into her day.[S1]

Researchers point to possible remedies. Bettini and her research partners found that supportive administrators and adequate planning time can help make the work feel more sustainable. Research has also found that "grow-your-own" programs, which recruit people who already live in the community, may be effective at filling special education jobs. NPR describes these approaches as possible ways to improve staffing, without establishing that any one measure would eliminate the shortage.[S1]

NPR said it set out to hear directly from about a dozen special educators, who have been sharing snippets of their lives through voice memos since the start of the school year, and that some will open their classroom doors to NPR in the coming months. That means the account is a snapshot of a small group of teachers rather than a statistical survey, and the broader figures come from outside research and federal data cited in the reporting.[S1]

Why it matters

Special education serves more than 8.2 million public school students, about one in six, and shortages are reported in 45 states. How schools staff these classrooms affects legal compliance on IEPs, the quality of services for students with disabilities, and the workload carried by a relatively small group of teachers.

What happens next

NPR plans to continue its special education diaries, with some teachers expected to open their classrooms to reporters in the coming months. Separately, states and districts will keep making staffing and pay decisions, and the gap between teacher preparation output and annual departures is likely to persist unless recruitment or retention changes.

What to watch

  • Whether more states add extra pay for hard-to-staff subjects like special education, reversing the decline from 22 to 17 states
  • District hiring of dedicated IEP paperwork staff, as at Boys' Latin of Philadelphia, and whether the practice spreads
  • Enrollment figures for special education students in the next federal data release
  • Growth of grow-your-own recruitment programs and their effect on vacancies
  • Whether planning-time adjustments negotiated by teachers like Fitzgerald become formal district policy
