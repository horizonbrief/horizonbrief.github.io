Trump escalates anti-Muslim attacks on El-Sayed in Michigan Senate race

Remarks at a Port Huron rally drew criticism from a former Arab American outreach ally, while El-Sayed rejected loyalty allegations in the Senate contest against Mike Rogers.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

President Donald Trump sharpened his anti-Muslim attacks against Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed on Sunday, casting him as a national security danger and impugning his allegiance to the country, according to an Associated Press report carried by NPR. The remarks, delivered in Port Huron, drew a direct rebuke from a founder of the group that helped organize the president's 2024 outreach to Arab American voters.[S19]

Trump described El-Sayed, a former Detroit health official, as a "jihadist" aligned with terrorists and said the candidate would "have to go through a metal detector before he's allowed to go into the Senate." The president returned repeatedly to the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks, likening the effort to keep El-Sayed out of the Senate chamber to the passengers who resisted the hijackers on United Flight 93.[S19]

El-Sayed would become the first Muslim senator in U.S. history if he wins. He has described questions about his loyalty as "shameful" and has denied any connection to extremist groups. At a rally in Grand Rapids later on Sunday, he brushed aside the president's habit of mocking his name.[S19]

The criticism from Bishara Bahbah stood out because of his role in building Trump's Arab American support. Bahbah, a founder of Arab Americans for Trump, said the president's language was unacceptable to Arab and Muslim communities, Christian Arabs included. His organization has since been renamed Arab Americans for Peace. The statement put a onetime architect of the president's outreach effort publicly at odds with rhetoric aimed at a Muslim candidate in a state where that outreach had produced gains.[S19]

The location of Trump's remarks added political weight. Port Huron sits on the Canadian border roughly an hour north of Detroit, close to communities where the president improved his standing with Arab American voters in 2024. In Dearborn, many voters had turned against the Biden administration over its handling of the war in Gaza, and their shift helped Trump win Michigan. Sunday's appearance thus placed the president's language in the very region where his coalition had recently expanded.[S19]

Republicans have spent the closing weeks of the campaign trying to tie El-Sayed to the Muslim Brotherhood, an Islamic movement with some branches designated terrorist organizations by the U.S. government. The AP report notes that no evidence supports any link between El-Sayed and the group. Republican nominee Mike Rogers repeated those allegations in remarks before Trump took the stage on Sunday, keeping the claim in circulation on the same day the president escalated his own attacks.[S19]

Loyalty had already become a central theme of the contest. In the second and final general election debate, Rogers pressed El-Sayed on the question directly, asking, "Are you for America, or are you for these terrorist groups overseas?" El-Sayed called the line of questioning "shameful" and invoked the Constitution. "I'm proud of our Constitution that guarantees all of us the right to pray as we choose to pray," he said. The exchange fixed allegiance as a defining issue well before the president's Port Huron remarks amplified it.[S19]

The AP account also described controversy over El-Sayed's own previous statements. It said he had repeatedly declined to say whether Iran or Israel was a greater threat to the country. Following an attack on a Detroit-area synagogue earlier in the year, he had used the phrase "hurt people hurt people." AP reported that an Israeli airstrike in Lebanon had killed four relatives of the attacker a week earlier. El-Sayed subsequently apologized, saying he had not meant to justify the attack and was sorry if his remarks had caused hurt. Those previous statements were a separate controversy from the alleged Muslim Brotherhood affiliation, for which the report found no evidence.[S19]

The Michigan contest between El-Sayed and Rogers is one of a handful of Senate races that could decide which party controls the chamber. The two are competing for the seat being vacated by retiring Democratic Sen. Gary Peters, in a bitter contest.[S19]

At the Grand Rapids rally, Sen. Bernie Sanders argued that Trump and his allies were resorting to Islamophobia because they have "nothing to say" about the concerns that matter most to working people, naming health care, housing and income inequality. Sanders' appearance alongside El-Sayed on the same day as the president's remarks illustrated how far outside Michigan the race has reached in its final stretch.[S19]

Analysis and outlook

Analysis and outlook are interpretations and possible future developments, separate from the reported facts.

Why it matters

The president's remarks push religious identity and loyalty questions to the center of a Senate race that could decide chamber control. Because El-Sayed would be the first Muslim senator if elected, the language used against him resonates beyond Michigan, framing a statewide contest around belonging as much as policy. The criticism from a founder of the group that led Trump's 2024 Arab American outreach suggests the rhetoric may carry a cost with a constituency the president courted and partly won in the state.

What happens next

The closing weeks are likely to see both campaigns sharpen their messages. El-Sayed's Grand Rapids response, converting the name-mocking into a "senator" line, suggests his campaign intends to turn the attacks into motivation rather than litigate them. Rogers had already repeated Muslim Brotherhood allegations before Trump spoke, so those claims are likely to remain a fixture of Republican messaging. Whether Bahbah's criticism translates into measurable erosion among Arab American voters is an open question that polling may clarify closer to Election Day.

What to watch

Watch whether other Republican figures echo or distance themselves from the president's language, and whether Arab American and Muslim community leaders in Dearborn and nearby areas respond publicly. Also watch the candidates' remaining appearances and closing advertising, particularly whether the Muslim Brotherhood claim persists without evidence. Fundraising and turnout signals in Michigan's Arab American communities, and any polling of that bloc, will indicate whether the 2024 shift toward Trump holds.

Background

Trump won Michigan in 2024 after making inroads with Arab American voters, including in Dearborn. Many voters there were angered by the Biden administration's handling of the war in Gaza. Arab Americans for Trump, later renamed Arab Americans for Peace, helped lead that outreach. The Michigan Senate seat is open because Democratic Sen. Gary Peters is retiring, and the El-Sayed-Rogers contest is among a handful that could determine Senate control. El-Sayed, a former Detroit health official, has repeatedly denied extremist ties.

Impact

The immediate impact is to nationalize a state race around the president's rhetoric, potentially energizing both El-Sayed's base and voters motivated by the attacks. For Arab American and Muslim communities, remarks from a president they partly backed in 2024 may deepen alienation, though the evidence so far is a single ally's criticism rather than polling. For Rogers, the episode ties his campaign to the president's language even as he advances his own Muslim Brotherhood allegations. The broader effect may be to test how much religious identity and loyalty framing shape a competitive Senate contest.

Trend analysis

The episode fits a pattern in which Trump uses inflammatory characterizations of opponents to dominate news cycles and consolidate support, while opponents try to convert the attention into turnout. It also illustrates the fragility of the 2024 Arab American realignment: gains built on discontent with the previous administration can be tested when the president's own rhetoric targets that community's representatives. If criticism from within his outreach coalition grows, Republicans may face a tradeoff between base mobilization and holding newer immigrant and Muslim voters. Much remains uncertain, including whether the language moves polls or merely frames the race's closing narrative.
