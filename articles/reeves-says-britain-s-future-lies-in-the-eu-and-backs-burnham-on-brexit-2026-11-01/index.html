Reeves Says Britain's Future Lies in the EU and Backs Burnham on Brexit

The former cabinet minister used a Sunday Times article to call Brexit a drag on Britain's economy and security, while urging re-entry supporters to make the case carefully and leaving any fundamental change to a future parliament and the public.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Rachel Reeves has said she believes Britain's future lies in the European Union and welcomed what she described as Andy Burnham's leadership on the question, writing in the Sunday Times that "Brexit has made us poorer, weaker and less secure." The intervention from the Labour former cabinet minister, published on Sunday, places a senior party figure explicitly behind the argument for closer EU ties at a moment when remarks by the prime minister have revived speculation about whether Britain could return to the bloc or adopt its freedom of movement rules.[S8]

Reeves did not attach a timetable or a mechanism to her position. She said it was "incumbent on supporters of our re-entry to make the argument, demonstrate tangible benefits and to get the next steps right" — a formulation that endorses the destination while conceding that the route is contested and that the case has to be won rather than assumed. That distinction matters in a debate where the gap between a long-term aspiration and a practical policy programme has repeatedly proved difficult to bridge.[S8]

Her endorsement of Burnham is the other half of the intervention. Burnham, the mayor of Greater Manchester, has set out a position in a letter to his cabinet stating that any "fundamental change" to post-Brexit arrangements would be for the public to decide and would be left to the next parliament. That framing pushes the decisive question past the current term and into a future electoral cycle, and it places the electorate, rather than any single party leadership, at the point where a change of direction would be authorised.[S8]

The immediate trigger for the renewed discussion, according to the Guardian's account, was remarks by the prime minister in recent days that fuelled questions about the prospect of Britain returning to the bloc or adopting its freedom of movement rules. The reporting does not establish that any policy change has been adopted or is imminent; what it describes is a shift in the terms of public debate, with senior Labour figures now willing to state a view on the long-term direction of the relationship.[S8]

Reeves's language is unusually direct for a former cabinet minister speaking on the record. Calling Brexit a cause of Britain being "poorer, weaker and less secure" bundles economic, diplomatic and defence claims into a single sentence. The economic element is the most familiar terrain of the post-referendum argument, and it is also the most contested: the effect of leaving the EU on growth, trade intensity and investment has been the subject of sustained dispute among economists, and the reporting here does not supply new figures or a new assessment. The security element connects to a separate and more recent strand of policy, in which the UK and the EU have been building defence-industrial links.[S8]

That strand is concrete. The Safe scheme, announced in March, is part of an EU-wide effort to rearm following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. A defence pact agreed with the EU in May paved the way for UK-based defence companies to participate in projects funded by the scheme. The arrangement is a working example of the kind of structured cooperation that sits outside full membership, and it gives Reeves's "less secure" claim a specific referent: the argument is not only about trade barriers but about whether a country outside the bloc can reliably sit inside European defence procurement and planning.[S8]

For Labour, the political management is delicate. A former cabinet minister endorsing EU re-entry, even conditionally, creates a marker that opponents can use to characterise the party's intentions, while supporters of closer alignment can cite it as evidence that the leadership's caution is not the whole story. Burnham's formulation — public decision, next parliament — offers a way to hold both positions at once: it validates the goal without committing the current government to a referendum or a negotiation it has not announced. Reeves's article adopts that structure, pairing a clear statement of belief with a procedural caveat about making the argument and getting the steps right.[S8]

The unanswered questions are substantial. The reporting does not say what "re-entry" would mean in practice — full membership, a customs arrangement, alignment on specific rules, or something else — nor whether freedom of movement, the issue the prime minister's remarks apparently raised, would form part of it. It does not specify what "tangible benefits" Reeves has in mind or how they would be demonstrated. And it does not indicate whether the prime minister's recent comments reflected a deliberate opening or an offhand remark that others have since interpreted.[S8]

What the reporting does establish is a sequence: remarks by the prime minister prompted questions about returning to the bloc or adopting freedom of movement; Burnham wrote to his cabinet setting out that fundamental change would be a matter for the public and the next parliament; Reeves then wrote in the Sunday Times welcoming Burnham's leadership, stating her view that Britain's future lies in the EU, describing Brexit's costs, and urging re-entry supporters to make their case properly. Each step moves the debate further into the open without producing a policy commitment.[S8]

The defence dimension may prove the most tractable ground for the argument Reeves is making. The Safe scheme and the May defence pact show that participation in EU-funded projects is already being negotiated on a sectoral basis, and that UK firms stand to gain or lose depending on how those arrangements are structured. If the case for closer ties is to be made through "tangible benefits," defence procurement is one area where the benefits and the costs can be identified with some precision, unlike aggregate claims about growth.[S8]

The economic claim is harder to pin down in a short article, and Reeves's sentence asserts rather than demonstrates it. That is a political choice as much as an evidential one: the phrase is designed to be memorable and to frame the argument, not to settle it. Readers looking for the underlying analysis will not find it in the reported remarks, and the absence of new data means the claim rests on the broader body of post-referendum economic argument rather than on anything disclosed here.[S8]

For now, the significance of the intervention lies in who is making it and how. A former cabinet minister stating that Britain's future is in the EU, and doing so alongside an endorsement of a mayor who has positioned any fundamental change beyond the current parliament, signals that the internal argument inside Labour about the direction of travel is being conducted in public. Whether that argument produces a programme, a referendum commitment or nothing more than a set of markers for a future leadership contest is not something the reporting resolves.[S8]

Why it matters

A former Labour cabinet minister going on the record that Britain's future is in the EU changes the internal cost of saying so. It gives pro-alignment figures a senior reference point, gives opponents a clear target, and shifts the question from whether the direction can be discussed to who owns the argument and on what timetable. What happens next

Expect the prime minister's office to be pressed to clarify whether recent remarks signalled a policy opening or were incidental. Burnham's letter sets a procedural marker — public decision, next parliament — that is likely to be tested by journalists and rival parties seeking a firmer commitment. The defence track is the most likely place for tangible follow-through, since the Safe scheme and the May pact create identifiable participation questions for UK firms.
