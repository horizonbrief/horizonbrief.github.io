Data centers become a midterm flashpoint — even in Brooklyn

A TechCrunch account of a New York election event finds AI and data-center backlash surfacing far from the server farms, as a report counts nearly $200 billion in projects delayed, stalled or blocked in the first half of 2026.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Data centers and artificial intelligence have moved into the midterm election conversation in places where neither is easy to see, according to a first-person account published by TechCrunch on October 31. Reporter Dominic-Madori Davis attended a midterm election event in Brooklyn hosted by Newsreel, the media company founded by former Forbes and Newsweek reporter Jack Brewster, five days before the November 3 vote. The room was filled with media figures, tech workers and policy advocates — an audience not obviously primed for a fight over server farms. Yet the subject kept coming up.[S24]

Brewster told Davis that AI and data centers "came up in every conversation on stage" and were the second most common topic in questions attendees submitted when they RSVP'd. He framed the finding as notable precisely because New York is not a place where data centers are very visible. "AI has become an election issue for regular voters," Brewster said, according to the account.[S24]

The event's stated aim, in Brewster's words, was for people to "put their phones away and talk to each other about the election, five days out." What they talked about, by Davis's account, was a set of grievances that connect national technology build-out to local politics: residents who believe large technology companies are cutting secret deals with local officials to build billion-dollar data centers, households squeezed by the cost of living, and anxiety about AI replacing workers or, in the more extreme framing voiced on stage, humanity itself.[S24]

The article anchors that anxiety in a figure. Davis cites a recent report from Data Center Watch finding that almost $200 billion in data center projects were delayed, stalled or blocked in the first half of 2026 because of political and community opposition. The report is described only in summary terms in the TechCrunch piece; the underlying methodology, the number of projects counted and the geographic breakdown are not detailed there, so the figure should be read as the article's characterization of that report rather than an independently verified tally.[S24]

On the panel, participants converged on an interpretation of what the facilities represent. Davis writes that the panel agreed data centers are "the physical embodiment of people's fears and anger toward the tech class and the rapid pace of technology." One panelist, identified as Flint, pushed back on the idea that host communities are simply weighing economic benefits. "What is a life worth?" Flint said when Davis raised the benefits some communities have seen from data center build-outs. People still protest, Flint said, because they "value their lives a little bit more than a temporary check."[S24]

Another panelist, Herndon, pointed to Ohio, where he said Vivek Ramaswamy was booed for making a similar argument about the tradeoffs of data center development. "I just want to reinforce the lack of consent happening," Herndon said. "Because that's true among Republicans in those places and I've watched it." Katz, another participant, was skeptical of the industry's employment claims, saying of the companies behind the build-out: "It's the same people who have taken so many union jobs away."[S24]

The account also captures the texture of a local political gathering. Along the warehouse walls, Davis noticed sticky notes answering posted questions, including "Will we ever be able to get Big Money out of politics?" One reply read, "God, I hope so." The article is written as a scene piece rather than a poll or a survey, and its evidence about voter sentiment is anecdotal: one event, one city, one host's account of the questions submitted. That limits how far the findings can be generalized, even as it illustrates how a national infrastructure story is being absorbed into local election politics.[S24]

Davis's own framing is that the gap between the technology industry's self-image and public perception is wider than people inside the industry tend to assume. She writes that she spends most of her days talking to people who see the benefits of AI and are optimistic it will succeed, and that talking to people beyond the tech world reminds her "how ugly it all seems to them." The piece also notes that AI came up near the end of one exchange, with a participant named Ossé saying he doesn't really use it — and arguing that when CEOs talk about a technology that could deplete 10% of human civilization, "they should be called to testify in front of Congress."[S24]

The Brooklyn event is a small data point in a larger pattern the article describes: data centers have become a ballot-box issue capable of swaying some midterm races. The mechanism it sketches is not a single national referendum on AI but a series of local fights over siting, disclosure and who bears the costs of power, water and land use — fights that can turn on whether residents believe deals were negotiated transparently. The Data Center Watch figure, if accurate, suggests those fights are already translating into delayed or canceled capital spending, not just rhetoric.[S24]

What the TechCrunch account does not establish is how much of the opposition is driven by data centers specifically versus broader cost-of-living and anti-tech sentiment, or whether candidates in competitive races are actually adjusting their positions. It also does not quantify how many voters rank the issue highly relative to inflation, housing or other concerns. Those are open questions that the November 3 results may begin to answer, but a single election night will not settle them.[S24]

For the technology industry, the practical implication described in the piece is that community consent is becoming a constraint on build-out timelines, alongside power availability and permitting. For local officials, the pressure runs the other way: projects promise tax revenue and construction jobs, while residents question long-term costs and the process by which deals are struck. The Brooklyn event suggests that argument is now being conducted in places with no server farms at all — which is part of why the article treats it as a midterm story rather than a local zoning story.[S24]

Why it matters

Data-center siting is shifting from a permitting and utility question into an electoral one. If opposition is now strong enough to delay or block projects worth nearly $200 billion in a single half-year, as the cited report claims, then build-out schedules depend not only on chips, power and capital but on local political consent. That changes incentives for developers — toward earlier community engagement, disclosure of deals and possibly higher costs — and gives local officials leverage they did not have when data centers were an obscure infrastructure category. Voters in places without visible server farms are also registering the issue, which broadens the political base for restrictions beyond host communities.

What happens next

The November 3 midterm results will offer the first concrete test of whether data-center and AI backlash moves votes or mainly shapes local races and ballot measures. Watch whether candidates who opposed specific projects outperform, and whether post-election coverage attributes outcomes to the issue. Separately, project developers may adjust timelines or announce community-benefit terms if opposition continues to translate into delays. None of this is settled; a single election night is a weak signal.

What to watch

  • November 3 results in races where data-center siting or local incentives became a campaign issue
  • Any updated Data Center Watch tally of delayed, stalled or blocked projects for the second half of 2026
  • Announcements from developers pausing, relocating or renegotiating specific projects after local votes
  • Local government disclosures or hearings on incentive deals that residents have called secret
  • Whether national candidates and party committees begin mentioning data centers or AI in ads and stump speeches
