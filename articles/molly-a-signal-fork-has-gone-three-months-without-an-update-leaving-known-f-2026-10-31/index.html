Molly, a Signal fork, has gone three months without an update, leaving known fixes unpatched

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Molly, a hardened fork of the Signal messaging app, has not shipped a release since 31 July, when version v8.19.2-4 was published, according to a community post on piefed.world dated 31 October. The post says the fork therefore lacks fixes that have already landed in mainline Signal, and it recommends that users switch to the official Signal client and delete Molly until the project resumes updating the app adequately, "if they ever will."[S6]

The claim rests on a single community write-up rather than a published security advisory, a coordinated disclosure or a statement from the Molly maintainers. The post does not name the specific vulnerabilities, cite CVE identifiers, describe the attack conditions under which they could be exploited, or say which mainline Signal versions contain the corresponding fixes. It also does not say whether Molly's maintainers have announced a pause, a handover or an end to the project. Those gaps matter for anyone deciding whether to keep using the fork.[S6]

The underlying concern is structural rather than exotic. A fork copies a codebase and then diverges from it. When the upstream project patches a flaw, the fork only benefits if its maintainers pull in the change, rebuild and publish. A three-month gap between Molly's last release and the current date means any security fixes merged into Signal during that window are, by the post's account, absent from the fork. The post frames the situation as one where fixes were "simply fixed" upstream while Molly "remains vulnerable."[S6]

Molly has been positioned as a privacy-focused alternative to Signal, and forks of messaging apps tend to attract users who want hardening or features the mainline client does not offer. That same audience is exposed when the fork's release cadence slips, because the security properties of a messaging client depend on staying current with upstream patches. The post's recommendation is blunt: use mainline Signal and remove Molly in the interim.[S6]

What the available material does not establish is the practical severity. Without named vulnerabilities, it is not possible to say from this source alone whether the unpatched issues require a remote attacker, local access, a malicious contact or some other precondition, or whether they affect the encryption protocol itself versus the app's surrounding code. The post asserts vulnerability but supplies no technical detail, and no second source in this evidence set corroborates the release date or the security assessment.[S6]

For users, the decision hinges on facts the post leaves open: whether Molly's maintainers have published a roadmap, whether a release is imminent, and whether the fork's build process tracks upstream closely enough that a delayed release still contains recent fixes. None of that is answered here. The post's own hedge, "if they ever will," signals that even its author is uncertain about the project's future.[S6]

The safer reading of the evidence is narrow. A community post says Molly's last release was 31 July and that the fork is missing fixes present in mainline Signal. That claim is unverified by a second source and undetailed as to the vulnerabilities themselves. Users who depend on the fork for privacy reasons face a trade-off between the hardening they chose Molly for and the patch lag the post describes, and the post comes down on the side of returning to the official client until the update cadence recovers.[S6]

Why it matters

Messaging apps carry their security in their update cadence. If Molly's last release really was 31 July, then every fix Signal shipped since then is a gap the fork's users carry, and the people most likely to have chosen Molly are those who care most about exactly this risk. The immediate effect is a trust and retention problem for a small privacy-focused project: users must weigh the hardening they wanted against patch lag they cannot easily audit, and the maintainers face pressure to publish a release or explain the pause.

What happens next

Watch whether Molly's maintainers publish a release, a roadmap or a statement addressing the gap. If a new build appears, the key question is whether it merges upstream fixes or only adds features. If silence continues, expect the community recommendation to shift further toward mainline Signal, and expect the fork's user base to shrink or fragment. None of this is settled by the current evidence.

What to watch

  • A new Molly release on its distribution channel, with a changelog naming merged upstream fixes
  • A statement from Molly's maintainers explaining the pause, a handover or an end-of-life plan
  • A published security advisory or CVE list identifying which Signal fixes Molly lacks
  • Community or forum threads reporting users migrating back to mainline Signal
  • Signal's own release notes for the period after 31 July, to gauge how much patching the fork has missed
