Main report

Shabana Mahmood will set out the final version of her plans to make foreign workers wait longer before they can apply for indefinite leave to remain, in a Commons statement expected after 3.30pm on Monday. The home secretary's announcement comes as Andy Burnham declined to say whether net migration is still too high, according to The Guardian's live coverage of UK politics.[S9]

The original proposals were published last November. Under them, most workers would have to wait 10 years rather than five before qualifying for indefinite leave to remain, while care workers would face a 15-year wait. The rules would apply to foreign workers already in the UK, not only to new arrivals. The Guardian reports that the broad thrust of the plan is being retained and that the rules will be tightened in a way that affects hundreds of thousands of people, but that relatively little is known about the final detail Mahmood will present.[S9]

That gap between the known outline and the unknown specifics is the centre of Monday's politics. The government has signalled the direction of travel for a year; what remains contested is the scope, the transition arrangements and how far the final version departs from the November 2025 blueprint. The Guardian notes that the detail, due after 3.30pm, will be crucial, and so will the reaction of Labour MPs.[S9]

Mahmood does not need primary legislation to implement the changes. That matters procedurally: without a bill, there is no committee stage and no chance for potential Labour rebels to amend the plans in the Commons. Opposition inside the party would have to express itself through statements, votes on other business or public criticism rather than through the usual legislative machinery. The Guardian reports that more than 100 MPs are queasy about what is being planned, a figure that, if accurate, would represent a substantial bloc inside the parliamentary party.[S9]

When Mahmood announced the plans last autumn, some government backbenchers were horrified, according to the Guardian. Public condemnation was mostly muted. The political context then was different: Reform UK held a lead of more than 10 points over Labour in the polls, and Mahmood argued that net migration was too high and that low-skilled foreign workers ended up being a burden on the taxpayer — a claim the Guardian describes as a key Reform UK argument that is contested by experts. The Guardian's assessment is that she was pushing at an open door at the time.[S9]

Burnham's refusal to be drawn on whether net migration remains too high sharpens a question the government now faces. The Guardian quotes him saying that with planned migration "it's difficult to say is it too high or too low, because it's got to be, if you like, following gaps in the economy." He declined to say whether it had to come down further. The Guardian's live blog frames the consequence bluntly: Mahmood will be defending plans drawn up in response to a problem that some might argue no longer exists.[S9]

What it does establish is the political asymmetry: the home secretary is finalising a restrictive settlement regime while a prominent Labour figure declines to endorse the premise that migration is still too high, and while more than 100 MPs are reported to have reservations.[S9]

The timing also intersects with a procedural grievance that has become familiar in this parliament. The Guardian notes that when the government has a big announcement, the Commons Speaker, Lindsay Hoyle, often erupts just before the relevant minister gives details to MPs, complaining that the main points have already been leaked to the media in contravention of the ministerial code. Whether that happens again on Monday depends on how much of the final package has already appeared in print. The Guardian's own account suggests the broad shape is known but the detail is not.[S9]

For the workers affected, the practical stakes are concrete. Indefinite leave to remain is the status that allows someone to stay without time limits and is typically a step toward citizenship. Extending the qualifying period from five to 10 years, and to 15 for care workers, changes the timeline for settlement, family life and career decisions. Applying the rules to people already in the UK, rather than only to new arrivals, extends the effect to workers who may have planned around the previous five-year path. The Guardian describes the impact as affecting hundreds of thousands of people.[S9]

The care sector is the most exposed group in the published outline. A 15-year wait for care workers would be the longest qualifying period in the plan, and it lands on an occupation that has relied heavily on overseas recruitment. The supplied material does not include government estimates of the workforce effect, nor sector responses to the final version. Those are gaps to watch rather than facts to assert.[S9]

Because the change does not require primary legislation, that unease has few formal outlets. The reaction after the statement — whether it is confined to private briefings or becomes public — will indicate how much political capital Mahmood and the leadership are spending on the package.[S9]

The final version arrives on Monday afternoon, after 3.30pm, in a statement to MPs. The home secretary does not need legislation. The detail and the Labour reaction are the two variables the Guardian identifies as decisive. Burnham's non-answer on whether migration is still too high adds a third: whether the government can still persuade its own side, and the public, that the problem the policy addresses is the one that exists now.[S9]

On the evidence available, the settlement regime is being tightened along the lines signalled a year ago, with the final scope and transitional treatment still unknown.
