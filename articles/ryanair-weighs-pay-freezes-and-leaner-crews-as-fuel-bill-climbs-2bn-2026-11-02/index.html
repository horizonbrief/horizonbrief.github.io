Ryanair Weighs Pay Freezes and Leaner Crews as Fuel Bill Climbs €2bn

Europe's largest low-cost carrier says jet fuel costs could rise by about €2bn next financial year and warns rivals may lift fares by up to 20%, as it reviews labour agreements, airport charges and crew ratios.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Ryanair is reviewing temporary pay freezes, lower crew ratios and a wider use of artificial intelligence across its group airlines as it braces for a roughly €2bn rise in jet fuel costs in its next financial year, the carrier said on Monday. Europe's largest low-cost airline also expects European carriers to raise ticket prices by up to 20%, according to The Guardian, a forecast that would mark a sharp shift for a sector built on stimulating demand with low fares.[S14]

The fuel estimate covers the financial year ending in March 2028. Chief executive Michael O'Leary tied the outlook to the Middle East conflict, saying: "Unless there is a swift resolution of the Middle East conflict we expect jet fuel prices to remain elevated well into 2027." Oil prices had hit a six-month high on Friday before falling to about $110 a barrel on Monday, the report said.[S14]

The cost review reaches across the group's airlines. O'Leary said Ryanair was "reviewing all costs across group airlines with a particular focus on airport churn negotiations, maturing labour agreements (which may require temporary pay freezes), lower crew ratios and increased use of AI." The company said the AI push would concentrate on productivity in operations and in back-office and support functions rather than on customer-facing roles, according to the report.[S14]

The timing matters because the measures are being floated alongside a profit decline. Ryanair reported post-tax profit of €1.61bn for the quarter ending in September, down 6% year on year but in line with analyst expectations. That combination — earnings still solid in absolute terms but slipping, with a much larger fuel headwind forecast for the following year — frames the review as preparation for a tougher cost environment rather than a response to an immediate crisis.[S14]

For passengers, the most direct signal is the fare forecast. Ryanair's expectation that European airlines could raise ticket prices by up to 20% implies the fuel shock may be passed through to travellers rather than absorbed entirely through cost cuts. Ryanair has historically positioned itself as the price leader in short-haul European travel, so a broad industry fare increase would test whether its cost base allows it to stay below rivals while protecting margins. The report does not specify the period over which such fare increases would occur, nor whether Ryanair expects its own fares to rise by the same amount.[S14]

For staff, the reference to "maturing labour agreements" and possible "temporary pay freezes" is the most sensitive element. Ryanair's labour relations across Europe have been contentious for years, with pilot and cabin crew groups in several countries organised into unions and periodic disputes over pay and conditions. The report does not identify which agreements are maturing, which countries or crew groups would be affected, or how long any freeze might last. Those gaps matter: a pay freeze applied to one bargaining unit in one market is a different industrial-relations event from a group-wide measure, and the report does not establish which is under consideration.[S14]

The phrase "lower crew ratios" is similarly undefined in the reporting. It could refer to the number of cabin crew relative to passengers on a given aircraft, or to crew numbers relative to flights operated. Regulatory minimum crew requirements set a floor in most jurisdictions, and the report gives no indication of whether Ryanair is examining staffing above those minimums or something else. Without that detail, the operational and safety implications cannot be assessed from the available material, and the report does not say whether regulators or unions have been consulted.[S14]

Airport charges form the third leg of the review. O'Leary cited "airport churn negotiations," language consistent with Ryanair's long-standing practice of shifting capacity between airports and regions in pursuit of lower fees and better commercial terms. The report does not name specific airports or routes under negotiation, nor does it quantify expected savings. Ryanair's network decisions have historically moved quickly in response to airport cost disputes, so this element of the review could translate into route changes more visibly than the labour or AI measures.[S14]

The AI component is the least quantified. Ryanair said increased use of AI would focus on improving productivity in operations and back-office and support functions. The report gives no budget, timeline, headcount target or specific application. Productivity gains from back-office automation typically take quarters to show up in unit costs, and the report offers no basis for estimating how much of the €2bn fuel headwind such measures could offset. The cost review as described is a set of options under consideration, not a package of announced cuts.[S14]

The macro backdrop is the variable Ryanair cannot control. Its fuel forecast is explicitly conditional on the Middle East conflict, and the report notes oil's move to a six-month high before easing to about $110 a barrel. Jet fuel prices do not track crude exactly — refining spreads and regional supply conditions matter — and the report does not break down Ryanair's hedging position for the year ending March 2028. Hedging coverage would materially change how much of a spot-price increase actually reaches the airline's income statement, and the report does not address it.[S14]

Ryanair's scale makes its cost decisions a reference point for the wider European short-haul market. If the largest low-cost carrier concludes that fares must rise and costs must be squeezed, smaller carriers with less bargaining power over airports and thinner balance sheets face the same fuel bill with fewer levers. The report's expectation of industry-wide fare increases of up to 20% is consistent with that pressure, though it is a company forecast rather than an observed outcome, and the report does not say how Ryanair arrived at the figure.[S14]

The immediate question is sequencing. Ryanair has flagged a review, not announced decisions. Pay freezes would require engagement with unions and would likely be contested; crew-ratio changes would run into regulatory limits and safety oversight; airport negotiations would surface as route and capacity announcements; and AI-driven productivity would show up, if at all, in later cost reporting. The report does not indicate which measures are closest to implementation, and O'Leary's language — "may require temporary pay freezes" — leaves the labour element explicitly conditional.[S14]

What is established is narrower than the headline. Ryanair forecasts a fuel cost increase of about €2bn in the year to March 2028, expects elevated jet fuel prices well into 2027 absent a swift resolution of the Middle East conflict, reported a 6% year-on-year fall in post-tax profit to €1.61bn for the September quarter, and is reviewing costs across airport negotiations, labour agreements, crew ratios and AI. What is not established is which of those measures will be adopted, on what scale, in which markets, or over what timeframe — and those are the details that will determine whether this becomes a modest efficiency programme or a more disruptive restructuring.[S14]

Why it matters

Ryanair sets the price floor for much of European short-haul travel. If the largest low-cost carrier concludes that fuel costs require both higher fares and lower unit labour costs, smaller rivals with weaker airport bargaining power and thinner balance sheets face the same fuel bill with fewer options. For travellers, the practical effect is that the era of aggressive fare stimulation may give way to fare increases across the segment. For crew and ground staff, the reference to maturing labour agreements and possible temporary pay freezes signals that wage negotiations across the group's airlines could become harder, though the report does not identify which bargaining units are exposed.

What happens next

The review is at the options stage. Watch for Ryanair to convert it into specific announcements: union consultations or ballot notices tied to pay, route or base changes following airport negotiations, and any disclosure on crew-ratio plans and their regulatory treatment. The fuel forecast is conditional on the Middle East conflict, so movement in oil and jet fuel prices — and any detail on Ryanair's hedging for the year to March 2028 — will determine whether the €2bn estimate holds, rises or eases.

What to watch

  • Any Ryanair statement or union notice specifying which labour agreements are maturing and whether pay freezes are formally proposed
  • Route, base or capacity announcements following airport negotiations, which would show where the cost review is being enforced
  • Disclosure on crew-ratio plans and whether regulators or unions are engaged
  • Jet fuel and crude price moves, plus any detail on Ryanair's hedging coverage for the year ending March 2028
  • Fare commentary from rival European low-cost carriers confirming or contradicting the up-to-20% industry increase forecast
