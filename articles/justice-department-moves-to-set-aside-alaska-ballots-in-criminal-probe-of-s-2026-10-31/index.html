Justice Department Moves to Set Aside Alaska Ballots in Criminal Probe of Senate Candidate

Federal prosecutors want certain ballots isolated while they investigate whether voters were confused by a Senate candidate who shares the Republican incumbent's name.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

The U.S. Department of Justice asked a court Friday to set aside certain Alaska ballots as part of an investigation into potential voter confusion, while federal officials confirmed a criminal probe involving a Senate candidate who shares a name with the Republican incumbent, NPR reported.[S5]

Alaska U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman told reporters in Anchorage that the investigation has been running for several months. He pointed to recent reporting by The New York Times indicating that Democratic candidate Mary Peltola's campaign appeared to be involved in recruiting a retired teacher named Dan J. Sullivan to run. That candidate shares a name with incumbent Sen. Dan Sullivan.[S5]

Heyman declined to say what crimes authorities are examining or who the targets are. He asked the public to come forward with tips. It is highly unusual for Justice Department officials to publicly discuss a criminal investigation or even confirm one exists.[S5]

The department also filed a list of proposed questions it wants to compel the Peltola campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee to answer. Those questions belong to a civil case seeking to block certification of the Senate race if the incumbent loses. President Donald Trump has said he directed the department to file that case.[S5]

Peltola denied Thursday that she knew anything about the effort to put Dan J. Sullivan on the ballot. "I am not interested in tricking voters," she said. At a campaign rally in Anchorage late Friday, she said she is "so deeply offended by these accusations about having any involvement with anyone else's candidacy," adding that she does not need help winning beyond the support of voters. Asked before the rally whether someone on her campaign could have been involved in recruiting Dan J. Sullivan, she told KTUU: "I have no idea."[S5]

An FBI agent visited a boat dealership in the candidate's hometown of Petersburg, according to its owner, who had donated to Dan J. Sullivan's campaign. The owner told The Associated Press the agent asked whether he had been misled by the candidacy; he said he had not, adding that people know who the incumbent is.[S5]

Justice Department attorneys in the Alaska case said there is a "substantial risk" that Dan J. Sullivan could be confused with the incumbent. Attorneys for the candidate, according to the reporting, have asked a judge to dismiss the case and said Alaska elections officials already took steps to distinguish the two Sullivans on the ballot.[S5]

The request to segregate ballots in Alaska follows a decision by the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals rejecting the Justice Department's request that California set aside disputed ballots received in the midterm election and sent to some 20,000 people the administration contends may not be U.S. citizens.[S5]

The timing of Friday's press conference is notable. The Justice Department has a longstanding practice of avoiding public steps in the weeks before an election that could be perceived as attempting to influence a political outcome. The Alaska race is among a handful nationwide expected to determine control of the Senate.[S5]

Trump, speaking at a rally in Florida late Friday, referenced Peltola and Dan J. Sullivan. According to the reporting, he said: "OK, he said 'yes.'" The context of that remark was not fully described in the account.[S5]

The state Division of Elections, in a letter dated Monday, said it signs agreements with local or tribal offices to run voting sites and that most of the 18 sites flagged in the reporting had opened. The account did not elaborate on which sites were at issue.[S5]

The sequence leaves several open questions. The department has confirmed a criminal investigation but not its scope, potential charges or targets. The civil case and the criminal probe are proceeding on separate tracks, and it is not clear how a court will rule on the request to isolate ballots or on the candidate's motion to dismiss. Peltola has denied knowledge of any recruitment effort, while the Times reporting described campaign involvement that she has not confirmed.[S5]

Why it matters

A public criminal probe and a ballot-segregation request in a Senate race that could decide chamber control put prosecutors' actions directly into an election's final days. That affects Alaska voters, both campaigns and the Justice Department's own norm of avoiding pre-election public steps. If a court grants the segregation request, it creates a mechanism for disputing results after the vote; if it refuses, the department's leverage narrows.

What happens next

A judge will weigh the ballot-segregation request and the candidate's motion to dismiss the civil case. Prosecutors may seek answers from the Peltola campaign and the DSCC under the proposed question list. The criminal probe could expand or stall, and the election outcome will determine whether the certification fight becomes live.

What to watch

  • A ruling on the motion to dismiss the civil case seeking to block certification
  • Whether the Peltola campaign or DSCC responds to the proposed questions
  • Any FBI interviews or public tips that surface in reporting
  • The Alaska Senate result and any post-election certification challenge
