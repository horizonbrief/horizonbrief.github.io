Data centre backlash grows as AI demand reshapes towns and grids

BBC reporting describes concern over power, water and noise alongside claims of jobs and tax revenue. A separate Apollo economist's assessment adds context on corporate AI returns.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Data centres have become the quiet infrastructure behind nearly every routine online activity, from search queries and email to streaming video and banking. According to BBC News, these warehouse-like buildings are also central to the artificial intelligence boom, housing the advanced chips and servers that run services such as ChatGPT and Claude. As the facilities grow larger and more numerous, the BBC reports that a backlash is building in communities that host them or are being asked to.[S1][S2]

The BBC cites Data Center Map, a company that tracks both operational and pre-operational sites, in putting the global count at about 12,250 data centres. The geography is uneven. The United States has the densest concentration, with 4,767 sites, followed by Europe, where the United Kingdom and Germany lead. Historically, the BBC notes, data centres have tended to sit near cities, where access to high-voltage power lines or water for cooling is easier. As the buildings get bigger, more are being sited in rural areas, shifting the debate from urban industrial zones to smaller communities.[S1][S2]

The BBC's example of scale is Colossus 2, part of the wider Colossus campus, which is expected to house hundreds of thousands of advanced AI chips made by Nvidia and to be used to train xAI's chatbot Grok. Elon Musk's company plans to use 41 methane gas turbines to help power the site. Activists quoted by the BBC say the turbines could "significantly increase" local air pollution, while local regulators say the turbines will meet air quality standards. The BBC juxtaposes the activists’ pollution concerns with regulators’ statements about air-quality standards.[S1][S2]

The BBC identifies resource pressure as a central driver of concern. Data centres can use significant amounts of water to cool their systems, which some experts say could lead to shortages. Their electricity demand is also growing, which the BBC says is driving up carbon emissions according to analysis, and putting pressure on local grids. Some fear that strain could push up energy prices.[S1][S2]

Political pressure is following. The BBC reports that 16 US states have proposed laws to ban or temporarily block new facilities, and that the issue could be a key one in November's midterm elections. Outside the United States, the BBC says opposition has emerged in countries including Mexico, France, Indonesia and the UK. The US measures are reported as proposals, rather than bans already in force.[S1][S2]

Industry advocates push back. The BBC notes that supporters say data centres are good for the economy, attracting investment, generating tax revenue and creating jobs in areas such as construction. The BBC also cites a recent report by the Brookings Center suggesting that data centres do create local jobs, although fewer than industry advocates claim. The type of facility matters, according to that research: so-called hyperscale sites, massive facilities owned and run by tech giants such as Amazon and Microsoft, were found to have a bigger positive effect on jobs, while "colocation" facilities, which lease space to remote tenants without a local presence, brought fewer benefits.[S1][S2]

In separate reporting on corporate AI adoption, TechCrunch described a note by Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, arguing that promised financial benefits have not appeared in the operating measures he examined. Slok focused on what he calls the S&P 493, the S&P 500 minus Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla, and found that profit margins and revenue per worker appeared flat, with no evidence that AI is improving productivity. TechCrunch reports that Slok sounded skeptical about whether corporate America can turn increasingly cheap intelligence into higher revenue growth and higher profit margins. His assessment concerns the operating measures of that company grouping, rather than a calculation of returns for individual data-centre projects.[S1][S2]

Taken together, the BBC reporting describes a planning and consent problem as much as a technology story. Facilities that power everyday internet use and AI services need land, power and water, and those needs are increasingly visible to neighbours. The BBC's account sets local environmental and cost concerns against industry arguments about investment, tax revenue and jobs, with regulators defending air-quality compliance at the Colossus site. The Brookings findings add nuance by suggesting that the local economic benefit depends on the kind of facility and its ties to the community, rather than being uniform across the sector.[S1][S2]

Why it matters

Data centres are the physical layer beneath AI and everyday internet services, so disputes over power, water, noise and local costs now shape whether and where digital infrastructure gets built. The BBC report shows the argument moving from technical planning to state legislatures and elections, while industry and regulators defend economic and air-quality claims.

What happens next

Watch whether the 16 state proposals advance into law or stall, how the issue surfaces in November's midterms, and whether operators adjust siting, cooling or power arrangements in response to local objections.

What to watch

  • Legislative movement on the 16 state proposals to ban or temporarily block new facilities
  • Whether data centres become a visible midterm election issue in affected districts
  • Local permitting decisions and community responses around large AI campuses such as Colossus 2
  • Regulator statements on air-quality compliance for on-site gas turbines
  • New research or disclosures quantifying water use, grid strain and local job effects
