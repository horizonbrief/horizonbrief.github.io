Japan's MMX probe heads for Phobos on sample-return voyage

The $578 million mission is in stable condition after launch, with a rover drop and up to two Phobos landings planned before a 2030 departure from Mars and a 2031 capsule landing in South Australia.

Horizon Brief summary

Japan has sent a $578 million spacecraft toward Mars's largest moon, Phobos, on a five-year round trip intended to bring samples back to Earth, Ars Technica reported. The outlet described the project as an international endeavor that could shed light on how the Solar System formed.[S7]

Once it separated from the H3 rocket, the MMX probe got in touch with ground controllers and deployed its solar arrays, Ars Technica reported. Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, the mission's project manager at JAXA, described the spacecraft as being in a "very stable condition."[S7]

Under the current plan, the Phobos survey phase would reach its peak in late 2028 or early 2029, when the MMX mothership releases a small rover to descend to the moon's surface. The mission's most demanding step is scheduled for later in 2029, when the main craft would try as many as two landings on Phobos.[S7]

Because Phobos's gravity is weak and irregular, a lander risks toppling or rebounding into space. Ars Technica reported that the probe's landing gear is built to take in the force of each touchdown to lower that risk.[S7]

After lifting off Phobos, MMX would jettison the landing gear and, in 2030, burn its engines to set a course for Earth. The following year, the spacecraft would steer a sample capsule down to a remote site in South Australia, according to Ars Technica.[S7]
