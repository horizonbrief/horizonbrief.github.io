Paulina Borsook's 'Cyberselfish' Returns in New Tin House Edition

TechCrunch reports that the 2000 book on techno-libertarian culture is coming back into print, with the author reflecting on Silicon Valley's leadership class and AI in a new interview.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Paulina Borsook's "Cyberselfish," a book first published in 2000 that went out of print, is returning in a new edition from Tin House, TechCrunch reported on October 25, 2026, in an article by Anthony Ha. The announcement arrives after a period in which the book drew renewed attention from readers and commentators seeking to understand how the technology industry "lost its way," according to the TechCrunch account.[S1]

TechCrunch interviewed Borsook over Zoom about the book’s revival and her interpretation of technology-industry culture. The interview provides her account of the ideas and workplace attitudes she set out to examine.[S1]

Borsook described the book's purpose in the interview as being "almost a travelogue" of her efforts to understand what she calls the techno-libertarian influence on Silicon Valley culture. She said she began moving through tech circles in the Bay Area in the early-to-middle 1980s, and that starting around the early 1990s she began hearing offhand remarks from some people that struck her as strange and that she could not make sense of. Those remarks, as she frames it, became the raw material for an attempt to map a set of beliefs rather than to write a conventional corporate history.[S1]

A central distinction in the book, as Borsook described it to TechCrunch, is between what she calls "Ravers" and "Gilders." The Gilders, in her account, are social conservatives, named after George Gilder, whom she described in sharply critical terms as a conservative and reactionary thought leader of the early 1990s. The Ravers, named in homage to John Perry Barlow, she associated with drugs, sex and rock and roll, a Burning Man sensibility and a more culturally liberal outlook. The point of the pairing, as she presents it, is that both currents coexisted inside the same industry culture, and that the libertarian strain she was trying to describe was not simply a matter of electoral affiliation.[S1]

On that question, Borsook was careful to separate culture from party politics. She noted that the San Francisco Peninsula still votes Democratic and that Ro Khanna is the area's congressman, and said she was really talking about culture rather than electoral politics. Her focus, she said, was the leadership class in tech, the people she calls the tech overlords, and she added that it is not so clear to her how much the rank and file share those attitudes, though she suspects probably more than when she was more familiar with the region.[S1]

Borsook also described employment insecurity as a reason some workers accommodate views they do not share. In her account, employees may decide to keep a job or a project while distancing themselves privately from the beliefs of their employers. She treated that compromise as a continuing workplace pattern, rather than a wholly new development.[S1]

Borsook criticizes a philosophy she considers strongly opposed to regulation. She told TechCrunch that the industry’s reliance on public research and development is missing from that account of its success. These are central arguments in her interpretation of the sector, rather than claims established by new research presented with the reissue.[S1]

Looking back, Borsook told TechCrunch that she sees more continuity than change. She pointed to the Netscape initial public offering as the moment things really began to change, describing Silicon Valley as having been invaded by finance professionals and a culture of flipping and fleeing. Even after the dot-com crash, she said, that attitude remained, percolating and spreading. She acknowledged, with what she called good reason, that a great deal of wonderful technology came out of the era and its successors, and said she would never deny that. Her criticism, she emphasized, concerns business culture and how industry leaders see themselves, rather than a denial that useful technologies emerged.[S1]

Borsook also applied her criticism to AI. In the TechCrunch interview, she compared the present AI moment to a Ponzi scheme. That was the author’s polemical characterization, not a legal or regulatory finding. Her remarks place current arguments over AI inside the longer critique of industry values she developed for the original book.[S1]

TechCrunch traces the revival to a sequence of events. Last year, Gil Durán, author of "Nerd Reich," publicly argued that it was time to revive Borsook's career, saying she "saw it all very clearly and tried to warn us 25 years ago." Later that fall, The New York Times reported that "Cyberselfish" was finding new fans interested in why the tech industry had drifted from its earlier promise. Borsook told TechCrunch that she began noticing used copies of the book commanding rising prices, along with unexpected social media posts from people she did not know describing the book as important, and inquiries from European academics. That attention, she said, eventually led to more press coverage and to the new Tin House edition, which includes a new introduction by Durán.[S1]
