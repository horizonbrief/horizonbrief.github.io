Binance says it has replaced 90% of $6.3bn hack losses as FBI blames North Korea

The exchange says users will not bear the cost of the record theft, drawing on its Secure Asset Fund, a futures insurance pool and corporate treasury. The FBI attributed the attack to a North Korea-affiliated group it calls TraderTraitor.

THE CONTEXT MATTERS

Main report

Binance said it has replaced 90% of the assets stolen in a $6.3bn hack of its platform, and that the remaining 10% — less liquid tokens that may take longer to buy back — will also be covered from company funds. The exchange put the amount it had set aside to absorb the losses at around $6.34bn, and co-CEO Richard Teng said users "will not bear the financial impact of this incident."[S20]

The disclosure, made in a post on X on Saturday, came the same day the FBI confirmed that a North Korea-affiliated group known as TraderTraitor was behind the attack. The bureau's attribution followed earlier suspicions from private investigators and analysts who had pointed toward Pyongyang-linked actors. Binance's statement did not dispute that finding.[S20]

The mechanics of the reimbursement matter as much as the headline figure. Binance said the money used to re-buy the stolen tokens came from three places: part of its $1bn Secure Asset Fund for Users, a bitcoin reserve the company holds to protect customers in the event of security breaches; some of its Futures Insurance Fund; and its own corporate treasury. The exchange described the 10% still outstanding as less liquid, meaning the tokens may take longer to acquire in the market. Teng said the company was "committed to covering the remaining 10%."[S20]

The scale is without close precedent in the crypto industry. A $6.3bn theft exceeds the nominal size of most previous exchange breaches by a wide margin, and the fact that Binance says it has already replaced nine-tenths of the losses in tokens rather than in cash or IOUs is a claim about its balance sheet as much as about customer protection. Drawing on the Secure Asset Fund — a reserve explicitly marketed as a buffer for security failures — converts a promise into an accounting event. Using the Futures Insurance Fund and corporate treasury alongside it suggests the reserve alone was not enough to cover the gap, though Binance did not break down how much came from each pool.[S20]

The FBI's attribution places the hack within a broader pattern of North Korean state-linked cryptocurrency theft that Western governments have tracked for years. The bureau's use of the name TraderTraitor ties this incident to a label US agencies have previously applied to North Korean hacking activity targeting crypto firms. The Guardian reported that the sheer scale of the hack has put a spotlight on North Korea's capacity to find illicit means to capture revenue, given the extensive international sanctions it faces over its nuclear and missile programmes. Crypto theft has become one of the more visible channels through which sanctioned states can convert digital assets into hard currency, though the specific laundering route used in this case has not been disclosed in the reporting available.[S20]

The timing of the two statements — Binance's coverage update and the FBI's attribution, both on Saturday — compresses what would normally be a longer sequence of disclosure, investigation and official confirmation into a single news cycle. Binance's post framed the reimbursement as substantially complete; the FBI's statement supplied the identity of the alleged perpetrator. Neither document, as reported, explained how the attackers got in, when the breach began, or how much of the stolen crypto has been traced or frozen. Those gaps are not incidental. They determine whether the incident is best understood as a one-off security failure at a single exchange or as evidence of a sustained campaign that other platforms should expect to face.[S20]

Binance's funding structure is worth examining on its own terms. The Secure Asset Fund for Users was set up as a bitcoin-denominated reserve intended to protect customers in the event of security breaches — a form of self-insurance that the exchange has publicly promoted. The Futures Insurance Fund serves a different purpose, typically associated with covering losses in derivatives trading rather than spot custody failures. Using it here, alongside corporate treasury money, indicates that the company assembled coverage from multiple internal pools rather than relying on a single dedicated reserve. That is a legitimate way to make customers whole, but it also means the incident has drawn on buffers that serve other functions, and Binance has not said how it will replenish them.[S20]

For customers, the immediate message is that balances are being restored in kind rather than written down. For the wider market, the episode tests whether the largest exchange's reserve architecture — the Secure Asset Fund plus insurance pools plus treasury — can absorb a loss of this size without a capital raise, a change in fee structure or a hit to confidence. Binance's willingness to name the funding sources is itself a signal that it wants the market to see the coverage as pre-funded rather than improvised. Whether that framing holds depends on the buyback being completed and on the reserves being restored to their prior levels, neither of which the company has put a timetable to.[S20]

The 10% residual is the part to watch most closely. Less liquid tokens are harder to source quickly without moving prices, and the longer Binance takes to rebuild that position, the more its claim of full coverage depends on market conditions holding. Teng's commitment is a statement of intent; the proof will be the completed buyback. Binance has not given a timetable, and it has not said whether the remaining tokens will be purchased on the open market, acquired through over-the-counter arrangements, or settled some other way.[S20]

The attribution also carries policy weight. An FBI statement naming a North Korea-affiliated group gives the incident a national-security dimension that a purely commercial breach would lack, and it may sharpen scrutiny of how exchanges screen for state-linked intrusion attempts. It does not, by itself, change the reimbursement math. But it reframes the hack from an operational failure at one company into a case study in how sanctioned states raise revenue through crypto, a framing that regulators and lawmakers in multiple jurisdictions have been building toward for several years. The Guardian's reporting notes that the scale of the theft has drawn attention to North Korea's ability to capture revenue illicitly despite sanctions — a point that fits a longer-running concern among Western governments about crypto as a sanctions-evasion channel.[S20]

What remains unclear is substantial. Binance has not said precisely when the hack occurred relative to the Saturday announcement, how the attackers gained access, or whether any customer funds beyond the stolen tokens were affected. The company's statement frames the incident as fully contained from a customer-loss perspective, but it does not address operational details, the status of any internal investigation, or whether law enforcement has recovered any assets. The FBI's confirmation did not, in the reporting available, include an estimate of how much of the stolen crypto has been traced or frozen. Nor is it clear whether other exchanges or intermediaries that may have received the stolen tokens have been contacted or have frozen anything.[S20]

Binance's post said the company had replaced 90% of the amount stolen. The remaining work is the less liquid tenth, the disclosure of how the breach happened, and whatever law enforcement tracing follows the FBI's attribution. Those are the open threads behind a headline number that, for now, Binance says it can afford.[S20]

Why it matters

A $6.3bn theft is large enough that the response, not just the breach, becomes the test. Binance is using customer-protection reserves, an insurance pool and its own treasury to make users whole, which shifts the cost onto the company's balance sheet and away from depositors. If that holds, it strengthens the case that large exchanges can self-insure against catastrophic losses; if the buyback drags or the remaining 10% proves hard to source, it weakens confidence in reserve-based protection across the industry. The FBI's attribution adds a sanctions and national-security dimension: crypto theft is being treated as state revenue-raising, which raises the odds of tighter screening requirements for exchanges handling large flows.

What happens next

Binance is expected to keep buying back the remaining less liquid tokens, though it has given no timetable. The FBI and possibly other agencies may release more detail on TraderTraitor's methods and on any traced or frozen assets. Watch for whether Binance discloses how the breach occurred and whether the Secure Asset Fund is replenished after being drawn down. Regulators in the US and elsewhere may cite the attribution in pushing for stricter exchange controls.

What to watch

  • A Binance statement confirming the remaining 10% has been fully replaced, with a date
  • Disclosure of the Secure Asset Fund's balance after the drawdown and any plan to top it up
  • FBI or Treasury detail on TraderTraitor's access method and any frozen or recovered assets
  • Any regulatory action or new screening requirement aimed at state-linked crypto theft
  • Binance customer withdrawal volumes and any change in fee or reserve policy
