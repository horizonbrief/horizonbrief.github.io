Survey: Managers Back AI but Find It Harder to Gauge Staff Contribution

A Nielsen survey commissioned by Monday.com found senior leaders enthusiastic about AI yet spending more time reviewing output, reworking evaluations and bracing to oversee more agents than people.

Horizon Brief summary

Managers say they are keen on AI, yet the technology is making a core duty murkier: working out how much of an employee's finished product is actually the employee's. In a Nielsen survey of 1,700 directors and senior managers in seven countries, commissioned by the work-management company Monday.com, more than half of senior leaders reported that assessing staff work had become harder since AI was adopted, TechCrunch reported.[S506]

The survey points to the finish line as the problem. Work often reaches a manager already refined, so the manager cannot easily separate the person's contribution from what the tool supplied. Nearly half of those polled said they now devote more time than before to reviewing and steering their teams' output.[S506]

That friction sits alongside strong stated appetite for the technology. According to TechCrunch's account of the findings, 94% of the managers said they were eager to use AI to improve work, and many felt sure about where it would deliver the most value. Michal Miller Levi, who leads market research and customer insights at Monday.com, likened the mood to the early internet era of the 2000s, describing a comparable sense of magic around AI. Her comparison is the view of an executive at the company that commissioned the survey, not an independent assessment of the results.[S506]

The criteria for judging people are shifting with it. The survey found that 78% of respondents had altered how they evaluate employees since bringing in AI, giving more weight to how well staff write prompts, choose which tasks to hand to the technology and try new ways of working.[S506]

Managers are also putting AI into their own processes. TechCrunch reported that 46% use it to help shape decisions, while 39% of leaders draw on it when drafting performance reviews.[S506]

Oversight is emerging as a job in itself. Decisions about which tasks go to AI agents, checks on what those agents produce and intervention when they err all require a person, and the report describes this as a possible new layer of invisible labor.[S506]

The tooling picture is another source of strain. Fifty-seven percent of managers said their organizations now run more tools than before, and about 51% said those tools had become more interdependent and clustered since AI arrived. Just 29% called their AI infrastructure fully optimized and centralized. Managers instead face additional apps and integrations, plus shadow AI use as staff turn to tools outside approved systems.[S506]

The composition of teams is expected to change too. 58% of managers anticipate overseeing more AI agents than human employees in the coming year, and 46% are concerned about losing control of what those agents do.[S506]

Read together, the responses describe leaders who welcome AI while still settling how to measure its contribution, how to check its output and how to keep its use in bounds. The figures reflect self-reported experiences and expectations gathered in a single commissioned survey, as reported by TechCrunch, rather than measured productivity outcomes or a finding meant to represent every workplace.[S506]
